**systime 178+194 (july 13 shabbat)**
-------------------------------------

I have spent my time here, these last few weeks, in comfort and joy and also in work. I have interviewed a round dozen of those who live here; it does not feel right to me to interview those on the tour groups for whatever reason, though I have not shied away from the topic of the Century Attack when it does come up.

I have started to notice the ways in which those who spend their life here in the sim treat the idea of the Attack and how they differ from those who live outside of it. There is a unity to the opinions of those here that is not necessarily present in those who approach from the outside. Perhaps such is to be expected, though; those who live here live in community with one another. Even when they do not like each other, or their characters do not like each other — for there can fictional enmity even between friends here, if both consent — they are still in community with one another.

As such, I have found that a lot of the responses have been focused on that. They have been focused on the story that is the life of the people here. Azaria is missed. Greatly so! And Azaria is not the only one who is no longer a part of that story, for two others perished in the Attack.

There was a push by some, I was told, to give this a religious tone, to write these souls out of the story as a miracle. Hananiah and Micah, who now walk with God! Who now sit with Enoch of old who is called Metatron at the right hand of the Divine Ruler of the Universe!

Too many lost too much, though, for a good number of us also have lives outside of this sim, outside of this story, and those shied away from this idea. They did not want to come here and think Hananiah and Micah are those who walk with God, and when they leave, they know that their friends who are not a part of this story are merely dead. Merely gone forever and all that remains is a core in the Graveyard.

And so instead, it is said that Azaria died of a bad heart, that Micah died in a skirmish in Betlehem, and that Hananiah fell ill and died after a fever, and they were mourned in kind.

What I have been hearing from tourists has been far more oriented around the loss that one feels as an individual, or the anger one feels at the attack as an individual. There is not quite so much of a sense of a unifying grief. There is not a unifying factor, except perhaps within the context of a synagogue, when the attendees will say, “Ah, I have lost no one but one is now gone from my congregation and we all mourn.” They do not have the same cares that those within the story might have.

Here am I, though, with one foot in both worlds. I say this about the members of my tour groups, and I must add that it is not _less_ that they feel this way! It is not incorrect that they do not think of their losses as a lessening of a whole, because their lives are complete in their own ways, not in the more singular way that many here live.

This is what I think about as I sit by the fire with Havivah and she grooms my tail while I prepare the ingredients for a stew. She complained good-naturedly to me when first I arrived that I was too free with my use of her ingredients, that I was making food too luxurious for a humble spice trader such as her. She gently poked fun at me for being Simcha the wealthy, the rich, the fat. But she never did complain about the food itself, and she always ate all that I made.

We have found a comfort between us that for a few days was heading more romantic than I think either of us were comfortable with, but now, we are simply rejoicing in our friendship. There are long conversations, and nights where we keep each other warm. She has spoken of the pain of losing Azaria and I have spoken of my fears of everlasting overflow. I have found that she fits well in my arms and she has found that she enjoys setting to my pelt with a comb.

When I mention to her my thoughts on the singular nature of the unifying force of the story, here, and how that impacts how those present grieve, she hums quietly to herself, pausing in the way she draws the comb down along my tail.

“You aren’t wrong, I think,” she says, sounding careful in her choice of words.

I set down the knife from where I am paring parsnips and peer back over my shoulder to her.

Offering a weak smile, she shrugs. “I really think you aren’t wrong, Simcha, but I’m worried about thinking too hard on the topic.”

“Ah, I am sorry, my dear. I did not mean to dredge up painful–”

“Feh! No, don’t you fret,” she says, laughing. “I’m worried about how easy it is to fall into Azaria’s thinking, the feeling that this is some other holocaust.”

I relax and turn back to my work, as does she.

“I see,” I say down to the parsnip in my paws. “It is...ah, well, this is something that has come up for me before, in my thinking, during those times when I was overflowing. I was thinking to myself, _who can retell the things that befell us?_ I was thinking about our focus as Jews on the tragedy in our lives, yes? That our songs are such? That we think of...ah, that we think of the fall of the Temples and our time in captivity, the pogroms, the Pale, the Holocaust.”

She laughs and gives my tail a gentle tug. “Yes! See? You are sounding like Azaria now.”

The tug gets a squeak out of me and a dirty look tossed playfully over my shoulder. “Yes, but...ah, but I came down on the side of that being not _all_ that we do, for we speak also so often of joy, do we not? We talk of the joy of our lives and the sweetness of Torah.”

“Our Simcha, talking of joy!”

I laugh. “That was my Hebrew name! I am...ah, I was not inventive enough to pick a whole new one when I started here.”

“Simcha bat...?”

“Simcha bat Peres v’Rahel,” I confirm.

“Well, my Simcha, you need not worry your head about not being inventive. Havivah was my Hebrew name as well.”

We linger in silence for a moment. I finish cutting up the parsnip, and then scrape it and the celery that I have been cutting into the pot, bubbling already with a lamb shank and a handful of barley. I have as well in there rosemary and za’atar and a bit of fennel and I will probably add some sumac at some point. I am not sure yet. I cook by feeling.

Havivah startles me to awareness by asking, “If you’re What Right Have I, are you then still Simcha when you’re called to aliyah?”

I laugh and lean forward to stir the stew. “I did consider taking a new name after...ah, after I forked from Rav, but no, I followed her example. We are all still Simcha unless we need to differentiate, in which case...ah, in which case she is Simcha Shlishit, and I am Simcha Revi’it. The third and the fourth, yes?”

“But aren’t you the...” I hear her mumble to herself, counting. “Aren’t you the thirty-third and thirty-fourth? What of the others in your clade?”

I shrug. “Few of them have reengaged with Judaism as we have. Some might attend a service now and then — Slow Hours joined us for a little it before I came here, and True Name would show occasionally before she was killed, some others off and on, yes? — but...ah, but few are ever called up for aliyah, yes? Once or twice, perhaps, and...ah, and then we always start off with their common names: _Motes of the Ode clade, Simcha bat Peres v’Rahel_ we called when we thanked her for some of her work on the new bimah.”

“I still remember my bat mizvah,” Havivah says, and I can hear the smile in her voice. “It was _terrifying._ I was so afraid I’d mess it up! I was always so bad at Hebrew!”

“And now look at you!” I say, laughing.

I am too cozy here, sitting with Havivah and talking, and yet the bread we have purchased needs to be warmed, so I for an ephemeral instance to go and collect the leaves of pita and bring them back to the cutting board before merging down.

Something about the action draws a thought from Havivah. “If you merge back down to From Whence, will whoever is the Simcha Chamishit have to re-number? And everyone after them?”

_“Khas v’shalom!”_ I cry. I wrinkle my nose, trying to keep the grimace to myself by fussing with the bread, but I can feel my tail bristling out. “We tried that once, and never again.”

She laughs and leans forward, wrapping her arms around my middle and resting her head on my back, just by my shoulder. “Ay, Simcha, _zay mir moykhl._ That bad, then?”

I force myself back into calmness and, after a moment’s hesitation, relax into Havivah’s embrace, leaving the leaves of bread sitting on my lap as I hold her arms to my front. “We...ah, well, we had originally planned that I would go and try out this life that I had set for myself and see what I gained from it, and then I would merge back down, yes? Five years, we gave ourselves. By the time that date came around, though, neither of us were all that excited and...ah, and so I left a fork of myself behind, and she forked as well, just in case. When I merged down...ah, well, it apparently felt to that instance of Rav that there were two irreconcilable lives within her. She could not make it work in her head. She lasted only three days before she quit, and Rav’s backup fork became Rav herself.”

“You’re two different people,” she murmurs against my shoulder, “so that makes sense. I’m glad you’re still you, and you’re still here.”

Later, as we lay in bed together, full and warm, with her fitting so nicely in my arms and this thought of those years back, the only time I ever merged down with Rav, still in my head, I decide that I will return home soon. There is comfort here, yes, and joy, but I miss home. I miss Rav. All I can think about, all I can see in my mind’s eye, is the look that was on that instance of From Whence’s face, of discomfort and dysphoria as the identity that I had formed warred with the identity she had kept. She was not me. There was an upwelling of pain in her, and trauma.

Pain and trauma.

I think now that I am starting to understand what it is that I am unearthing in myself, what this feeling of change is, what has happened to me over the last year and a half.

One more week here, I think, and I will wrap up my planned tours and make sure that Havivah is prepared for my departure, and I will return home. I think of her expression that day, decades and centuries ago, and I know that I am less her than ever, and I miss her.
