I will stop just shy of saying that I returned with the idea for Yom HaShichzur fully formed in my grasp, but only just. I did not arrive with the idea in place, bit all of the conditions for it were there. Every precondition was met. All the pieces were in place. What was missing, then was the impetus.

Those preconditions at the time were too close for me to see for what they were. They were patterns that were not yet visible as patterns: the desire to think of the attack at the expense of the restoration, the desire to demonize phys-side and idolize sys-side, the desire to seprate — phys- from sys-side and attack from restoration — and thus oversimplify. All of these were evident even back at The Bean Cycle, even within myself, but they were so much more evident at Beth Tikvah.

What, then, was the impetus?

I was not alone in this desire, for my committee was quickly populated, but even at that time, we — the five of us in the committee — could not name it at first. All we knew is that there was a drive in us to push away from the need to memorialize the attack and instead to memorialize the return.

Nor were we alone. These patterns seemed universal. The feeds were rife with discussions that demonized various parties, all the way down from the individual attackers, to the collectives involved, all the way up to Earth, to all of phys-side. Even now, some SysCon working group circulates a steady stream of questionaires about what is to be done with the attackers, and I am disheartened to see the tenor of the responses.

“There is...ah, rather, this is one of the defining features of New Reform as a movement, though,” I said. I had to work to keep the growl out of my voice. There was no helping my expression; I knew how sour it must like look. So be it. My expression was sour.

Erin frowned, as though trying to match my sourness with her own.

“We recognize the different paths that life now takes, Rav Sorensen. We recognize the unique role that death plays. You and I have both written on this, yes? We have... Ah, we have workshopped each other’s essays in the MN, yes?”

“Yes,” she hazarded. So careful was her voice that I was reminded once more to moderate my tone.

“It is important that we hold to that, my dear. It is important that we, as...ah, as guides within this space remain focused on the fact that death is different. The Attack was important, the dead are dead, but our focus in the context of the ANRC should be also on the restoration. It should be on...ah, well, it should be on the deliverance from death. We will remember the dead, but we must also remember the living.”

“What Right Have I,” Rav murmured quietly. “I do not think that Erin is suggesting that we forget that we were restored. I do not think that anyone is. When we speak of a commemoration of the lost on New Year’s Eve, we are not trying to in any way lessen the impact of those efforts that brought us back.”

There was a part of me, then — and I recognize it now as petulance — that grew frustrated with Rav for so conciliatory a tone turned on me. Was I not already working to moderate my own tone? Was I really coming off as so angry?

“Indeed,” Erin was saying. “My only goal bringing this up is to prepare us for the mourning that’ll take place on that day. The yahrzeit readings that week alone will be overwhelming.”

I nodded.

“You seem like you’re angling for something else, though,” she said. Her smile was kind throughout, but I had to work to keep from bridling all the same. “Can you try putting in more concrete terms what you’re looking for?”

“I just...ah, I simply do not want this to turn into another Yom HaShoah. The Shoah did not give us this chance. It was...ah, it was terrible what happened, but wondrous that so many of us continue. That _we_ continue. The three of us, so many Jews, so many people!” I wrangled my thoughts for a moment, trying to stay on track for this request of concrete terms. “I do not want New Year’s Eve to turn into another Yom HaShoah. I want instead that...ah, that February 10th be celebrated. I know that many are speaking already of it as Restoration Day, and so perhaps we should lean into that. Perhaps it should have its own Yom Tov. Yom...HaShichzur, perhaps, yes? I know that...ah, that I am not on the board, but you two _are.”_

Both rabbis leaned back in their chairs. Both looked thoughtful, but both also looked...concerned? Tired? Was I really wearing them so thin?

At least, Erin sighed and gave me a wry smile. “Alright, What Right Have I. You up for another committee appointment? Maybe you can workshop the name some. That _–chz–_ in the middle is going to scare some people.”

I smiled faintly in return. “A committee, mm? You will consider it?”

She nodded.

Rav rested her paw atop my own. “I think we already are, my dear. That is what this conversation is, is it not? Already we are less heated.”

Some time later, after Erin had left, Rav and I retired to the cafe beside the synagogue. Perhaps we sought some less formal setting after that ad hoc meeting. It was not a committee, but at times I felt it may as well have been. From Whence had found a professional friend in Erin Sorensen, and she and I had, as Rav put it, complementary autistic tendencies. It was not a committee to do anything, and yet we accomplished rather a lot together, the three of us.

Viz me suddenly winding up with a committee to discuss a formal recognition of restoration day. Even if we had clashed some leading up to it, I knew that Erin would be true to her word and spin up a working group within the ANRC’s already expansive morass of committees.

And yet, I could not help but feel that I had been granted this boon as a way to shut me up. To placate me. To keep me happy. To get the noisy anxiety-skunk to pipe down.
