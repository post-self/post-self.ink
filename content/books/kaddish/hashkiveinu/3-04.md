This project of interviews and talking has been proceeding quite smoothly, and I have now collected my fiftieth interview, averaging about one every two days. This has, of course, been aided by the ability to conduct them in parallel; given the amount of time I have spent subsumed by overflow, I would never have been able to do so otherwise.

I am starting to find the threads in the responses that I have gotten, as well. This is one of the things that I feel good at. I like that I am able to pull together stories and start to find the trends. Much of my ThD was spent doing precisely this: interviewing — friends and acquiantances in this case, rabbis in the case of my degree — and then collating, coding, pulling stories out of the words and understanding all the better the way in which things work.

I will soon begin to work on the next step, which is writing. Soon. I do not know when. I do not know how many interviews will feel like _enough_ to me. Perhaps when my intervewees start to be strangers that I do not know.

Until then, I have been working on the project for much of the week, and reserving Shabbat for myself, as feels proper for me. I will work on this project, this journal, on Shabbat, yes, but not something that feels so much like work. Pleasant work, yes, but work. This feels far more immediate, and at times all the more pleasurable for it.

Some time ago, back when I was working on my ThD, I found that I was becoming increasingly distracted by the vagaries of life. There was so much to do! Beth Tikvah had its centennial celebration. I was working with several committees with the ANRC. I was studying, yes — reading and collating information — and attending lectures, but there was so much life to live! I at times would work in multiples, with two or three of me running around, accomplishing this or that, but when it would come to the end of the day and it would be time to reconcile, I would find that my sleep would be unrestful. There is, after all, the act of addressing merge conflicts, but there is also the processing that must take place above and beyond that.

And so when it came to writing, something done for pleasure or for school, I found that I was often quite frustrated by the sheer amount of stuff that needed doing, needed thinking about.

Much has been written in Jewish spaces about time, about space we give for events. We carve Shabbat out of our weeks. We give holidays a full twenty-five hours. We delineate quite well, with the setting of the sun or the falling of night.

But what of the little things? What of the hour I am to spend writing this or that? What about the very path forward that would help me with my degree? After all, a thesis is a thesis, and my thesis needed doing!

I tried many approaches to accomplishing this. Pomodoros were a natural for me, because there was a defined start and end time. I would write for twenty or thirty minutes, and be productive enough. I would have words down on the page, and that would be a success.

Something about this approach was strange and unnatural. Artificial. There was a sense of rectilinearity to it, of right angles where there should be some more artful curve. Starting began and ending finished. It was a square wave of effort, and my mind rebelled against such hard starts and hard stops.

I tried also dividing my day into chapters. Rectilinear still, and yet perhaps with softer corners, a bit more rounded.

I would say that mornings, my chapter one, were for self-care. I would rise, drink coffee, groom myself, and sit for a while in peace.

My chapter two, running from perhaps .40 to midday, was dedicated to school, for that is when most of the lectures were. It was natural, then, that chapter three, which ran until midafternoon, would be for reading and research.

Chapter four — which encompassed dinner — was for social obligations related to Beth Tikvah and the ANRC, for committee meetings often took place at the table.

Chapter five, then, which ran from mid to late evening until bedtime was nominally free time, but I was so bushed by then that I would often settle into a book or a partner's arms for the comfort that came with having pleasantly exhausted my resources.

What I eventually found that worked, however, was to treat writing, whenever it was to happen, as a ritual. I treated writing time as a spiritual endeavor. I might section out time regularly — I finished my degree in this way, though this required toying with my more strict adherence to chapters — or I might steal an hour here, a half hour there.

_Baruch atah, Mechaver Kadosh,_ I would chant to myself, _shotef b'maaseh hayetzirah._ Blessed are you, Divine Author, partner in the act of creation.

Then, each word would flow through me and my paw would be guided by that Divine Author and my breath would be the breath of creation itself.

With this journal, I have at times treated it with the same spiritual care. Some of these entries — this one, for instance — were written just as I might write a paper, a chapter, an essay. I will breathe deep and chant aloud or to myself my bracha, and then I will begin.

Sometimes, though, and this is a new feeling, I will simply think my thoughts into words and get them down. Rarely has writing ever felt so immediate and unguided, and there is, I am finding, a strange pleasure in that.

Perhaps there is more joy to be found in immediacy than I have given credit.
