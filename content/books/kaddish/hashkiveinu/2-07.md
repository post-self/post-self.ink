Having taken a few breaks in my reading (for the book is quite heady and not always interesting to me, and I have several other books on the go at the moment), I have finally finished Merton’s _The Sign of Jonas_, the book that Joseph gave me.

I suspect that, given time and energy, and more inclination than I have, I could write a proper review of the work, or even a response. A response! Had I not a goal in the writing of this — that it be a parallel, personal exploration of my thoughts alongside this project that Rav has provided me — then I suppose this project might well count.

But no, I do not have the time or energy, and certainly do not have the inclination. This project is my own. It belongs to a different time, a different faith, a different person. It is not a response to Merton, is not a contradiction, or in dialogue with him. I am writing a journal, and Merton wrote a journal, and we both live a religious life, and that is perhaps the start and end of the engagement between the two projects.

But oh, do I have thoughts.

First of all, I found precisely some of the frustrations that I expected. I found that particular Jews-killed-Jesus brand of antisemitism that was all but sanctioned in many Catholic spaces, only officially struck down in the Second Vatican Council.

This is perhaps part of a larger frustration that I have with the work, which is one that I have also experienced in my ecumenical labors even today, which is that I struggle to truly understand the centralized nature of Catholicism, and indeed many religions and denominations. The amount and shape of heirarchy and the flow of information feels so foreign to me!

Within New Reform Judaism, we have the ANRC — the Association of New Reform Congregations — which comprises representatives from all member congregations (often, but not always, the rabbi), who are eligible to vote and who may be members of committees or their offshoots, working groups. There is also a small staff both sys- and phys-side — Rav Erin Sorensen, for instance, is the chair of the ANRC, but is not a congregational rabbi — that manages some administrative and technical tasks. Information is passed around via hundreds (sometimes it feels like thousands) of mailing lists, and decisions are made either via a democratic process or via _she’eilot u’teshuvot_, or halakhic responses to questions posed to rabbis. This, along with helping to edit _Mishkan HaNetzach_, our siddur and guidebook, is my role within the organization. Indeed, the responsa are included as part of the siddur within the guidebook portion.

The ANRC, though, is a very flat organization, as are member congregations. Within the ANRC, there is a board tasked primarily with organizational affairs, and then the members. Within congregations, there are roles that people play, and sometimes those roles come with _some_ authority — the rabbi, for instance, is the first line determining halakhic rulings and other validities, but beyond that, they are a Jew who has studied for long enough to be trusted to do so; the cantor is a song leader who has been trained as such; and the president of the congregation is often a regular member of the temple who helps the committees organize with each other — but we are all just Jews.

Upon reading Merton’s journey, from postulant to novice to professed monk, through his solemn vows, becoming subdeacon, then deacon, then father... Hearing about the organization of just Gethsemani Abbey, then of the Cistercian order, then of the church...

I know that I am not built for such a life.

I am not built for dictats from on high. Few of the clade are, I suspect. We are anarchists to a one, and build our lives on cooperation and discussion. Even True Name — _especially_ True Name! — who had all sorts of accusations about being some sort of grand leader passing down dictats from on high, was just a particularly charismatic anarchist.

This feels to me very Jewish! This feels like a way of life that is part of our community beyond just our clade.

We have a spiritual responsibility to be part of the community, to _always_ be a part of the community. _Al tifrosh min hatzibur,_ do not separate yourself from the community, and it is a community that might not be, almost certainly is not, and probably _should not be_ uniform in nature. We discuss and argue and change and grow together, and it must be together.

Ah, but here is one part of the story where I found myself yearning right along with Merton: his desire for solitude.

I truly believe all that I just said about community, and about the ways in which we communicate within that community, and yet sometimes, I really do desire solitude. There is no eremitical tradition in contemporary Judaism. Perhaps that is good, for if there were, I imagine that I would have become a hermit and disappeared long ago.

Hah!

And what would I become, then? Would I get lost in books? Would I continue to write? Would I simply disappear from the world at large as some cladists do?

But even for the draw of silence, even for the desire for solitude, I do not think I could actually exist like that for long. There are still too many connections that are too important to me. My chavruta is too important, and my congregation. The members of the committees that I am on are precious to me. Rav is too important by far for me to withdraw wholly from her.

You see now the struggle? I want and I do not want solitude. I need and also cannot have silence.

Merton became a hermit later in life. He got the solitude that he desired in his contemplative life. I am at the same time envious of such and also understanding that such is not for me.

Now look at me. I have said that I was not going to write much about this book, and I have thrown together a thousand words. I think that, in general, _The Sign of Jonas_ has proven its worth if only that it has showed me some of what a journal like this — even with all its differences — can become. I must thank Joseph for that.
