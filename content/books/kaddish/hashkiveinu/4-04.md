**systime 278+280 (Oct 7, Yom Kippur)**
---------------------------------------

Night fell slowly, smoothly on New Year’s Eve, the shadow of the town climbing peacefully the hill on which Beth Tikvah sat. Orange light touched the grass, then the trunks of the trees, crawling up into the canopy. Orange sighed into red, purple. Earthshadow cast a dim wedge up into the sky. A few whispy clouds high up still held some pale light to them, yellows and golds and tans.

Torches and fairy lights eased into life around the shul and ringing the grassy lawn. They seemed to capture the warm colors of the sunset and hold onto them just a little bit longer.

“Golden apples of the sun,” I muttered.

“I am sorry, my dear, what was that?” Rav said. She had been sticking close to me throughout the afternoon and evening, her root instance helping me set up chairs, blankets, and tables out on the field, hauling food from the refectory up to set in chafing dishes. Even now, she had followed me up to the low rise that stood between the field and the building itself, the two of us looking out over the synagogue. These two women who had shaped themselves to be precisely what they wished, taking into account all of their differences, and yet who were somehow still so alike. I knew why, but neither of us said anything about it, not just yet.

““The Golden Apples of the Sun”, yes?” I repeated. “That story. The one where they fly a ship to space to fill a crucible with... ah, with the substance of the Sun to bring back to Earth.”

She turned to face me with a strange look on her face. The silence drew out, and I imagined she must be looking up this reference I had dredged up from the corners of my mind.

“What brought that to mind?” she says at last, a smile touching the corners of her mouth.

I gestured toward the torches, the fairy lights. “We captured little bits of the Sun, yes? It is... ah, it is our little attempt to hold back the night, is it not? To keep a bit of the day with us?”

She laughed, not unkindly, and slipped her arm around one of my own, leaning gently against me. I could feel the tension within her. “I suppose we did, yes,” she replied. “There is always that drive for just a little more daylight.”

I nodded.

“Today more than most.”

I nodded again. I stayed silent.

“What Right Have I? Are you alright?”

“Yes,” I said at last, “but... ah, but can we speak about this later? Perhaps after the fireworks?”

She straightened up to give me a searching glance, then nodded. “Of course, my dear.”

This was still some hours away. We drifted apart — but never far — in order to continue on with the night. We greeted friends and had conversations, grabbing plates and joining the line at the food, filling up on pasta salad and fried potatoes and keftes de espinaka, bourekas and deviled eggs and sweet potato pie. We enjoyed the fading light and the food, and always we were conscious of one another.

The mood was careful, as though the very night itself were fragile, as though it might crack at if we did not treat it delicately and spill in too much light.

There were fewer here tonight than there were, by our reckoning, a bit under a year ago when last we celebrated the New Year. Many, I knew, were at home, at the places where they felt safest. At home, or perhaps with loved ones, where it was quiet, where it was secure. Several had decided to attend a vigil of sorts. I had been invited, and had turned down the invitation as politely as I knew how.

It was joyful, here on the hill, but muted.

My joy bore the scent of determination. I know that some others felt this, too, there on the hill, that we were determined to celebrate the new year tonight as fiercely and freely as we did on the night of December 31, 2399.

This fact, too, I kept to myself, though. Part of that joy was in community, and there was no need to disturb the fragility for the sake of making a point.

So I made my way through the food line and piled a plate high with goodies and stayed close to Rav as she stayed close to me. We spoke with friends, laughing and giving hugs. I skirted around some groups that I knew to be emotional as Rav would occasionally drop in and offer comfort.

At last, a gentle chime rung out over the crowd, coming from over by the synagogue, and we all waved away our plates and moved to the seats and blankets that had been sent out, staying in families and groups of friends, clumped up with those we loved, and sat, waiting. Thirty seconds. Quiet murmuring, caution rising.

“Ten. Nine. Eight.”

Our voices were tentative at first, still cautious of the fragility of this moment of peace.

“Seven. Six. Five!”

When the peace did not shatter, we grew louder.

“Four! Three!”

Rav slipped her paw into mine. I gave it a squeeze and offered her a reassuring smile.

“Two!”

There were tears on her face and there was fear in her eyes.

“One!”

I held tighter.

The first firework arched up into the sky high above the shul.

And then two more. And five more after that. A sea of light. A winding, blossoming mass of color. Golden apples of the sun. Prometheus. The sky lit up and our shadows were cast starkly onto the ground and I turned my hearing down as our little bits of captured sunlight burst forth and we did not die.

We reclined in our chairs together, Rav and I, and although I could hear that her tears continued, her grip on my paw relaxed throughout the show.

After the finale, came a slow recitation of the handful of congregants that had perished in the attack. Ten months, two weeks, and four days ago, by our reckoning, they had disappeared, and yet here this was their second Yahrzeit, by the universe’s count.

Rav and I both bowed our heads in silence, mulling over our memories of these few. She doubtless had more than I did, given her position, but even I knew them well.

I held in my heart, however, that fierce joy even still. I held onto that until it burned me. I clutched it to my sense of self and did my best to let it suffuse me. I knew that this recitation was necessary, and I said Kaddish along with everyone else gathered there, but I also knew, now, that this was not to be the only acknowledgment of the attack, that the planning for Yom HaShichzur was already underway, along with however many other Restoration Day celebrations there would be, that event that began this whole project, however many pages ago. I knew that I had, in my own way, succeeded at my goals, and I knew also that those goals did not negate this particular need for remembrance that we had.

My joy was that I was alive. Yes, it was also that I had succeeded, but my joy was that I was alive, and I knew that recognition for this lay ahead.

Bit by bit, the field emptied, with individuals, groups, and families either stepping off toward home or wandering down the hill to the small town that makes up part of Beth Tikvah. Rav and I wandered throughout, separating as before, moving apart but with an eye to our barycenter. Me to keep an eye on her, and her to stay by something known, perhaps. I realize, now, writing this, that I am not totally sure what drove her toward me that night. I have ideas, yes, based off what I know of her, but I am not totally sure.

And then, it was just us and a few stragglers. We walked among the chairs, waving the empty ones away as we came across them, and then down along the line of picnic tables that bore the food, waving away the dishes and the canopies, and then then tables themselves so that we could leave the field as it had been before.

And we spoke as we walked.

“Tell me, my dear,” she said, “why it is that you thought of “The Golden Apples of the Sun”.”

“You mean... ah, you mean other than the torches and lights?”

She nodded. “You said that you would prefer to talk about it later, and so I am wondering if your thoughts are more settled.”

“They have been settled for some time,” I replied, chuckling. “But... ah, well, I did not wish to bring our night away from the joy that it held, or the memories that we all had, yes?”

She smiled and gave a hint of a bow in acknowledgment.

“You actually... ah, you actually hit quite close to home when you guessed that there was a drive for a little bit more daylight. I was thinking of our drive for a little bit more life.”

“I took a read of the story while we were watching the fireworks, and I was wondering how much it actually fit.” She snorted. “The 1950s! I knew it had to be more on the metaphorical side.”

I laughed. “Of course, yes. It is... ah, it is a servicable story with a servicable poem. I do not even know why it came to mind, other than the title and the idea of capturing a bit of the sun. I was thinking of the ways that... ah, that we were hoping for a bit more day, a bit more life, and–” Seeing her tense only slightly, I carefully guided my sentence moving forward. “–ah... and I am glad that we get that much more, here. This is our home, and I imagine that we each make it complete for the other, too, yes?”

The tension drifted out of her shoulders and she turned her face away from me, looking over toward the shul and her apartment. “Yes,” she said, voice thick. “Yes, we make it complete for each other. Thank you, What Right Have I. I am glad you are here.”

“As I am you,” I said, tamping down a tic at the clumsy words. They could stand as they were. I could let them be.

We finished waving away what we could, offered a few goodbyes to stragglers, and began to make our way over to Rav’s apartment. At the door, she turned, took both my paws in hers and smiled to me.

“You know, I think I am now quite glad with your choice,” she said.

“My choice?”

“Your choice to focus on Yom HaShichzur.”

“But you–”

“I know, I said that I agreed with you,” she interrupted gently, laughing. “I still do, even. What I mean to say is that I am pleased with your choice to focus on this and not the alternative. I like _this_ you, my dear. I like this you focusing on still being around.”

_You like that I did not become you._

The thought bubbled up uncharitably and sat just below my sternum, an uncomfortable pressure, a psychic indigestion. It was not true. It was not true and it most certainly was not fair. When had I become this thing? This unthinking, judgmental, petulant thing?

I only smiled to Rav, begging off any further conversation as being too tired so that I could return to my apartment and lay awake in bed, wondering.

Ah, would that I had not been so deep in overflow over Pesach to dwell in such memories! Would that I had been more grounded even on Pesach Sheni. I missed even my second chance to celebrate Passover at a point in time when I am not wallowing in overflow. Would that my mind had cooperated! What a stroke of luck that would have been, what a joy provided by my ancestors! I would have had the time to sing _Dayenu_ and have it be a song about escaping Egypt, receiving the Torah.

But perhaps I still should, at that. _Illu hotzianu miMitzraim, dayenu..._ If Adonai had brought us out of Egypt, it would have been enough.

Egypt.

_Mitzraim._

The Narrow Places.

We teach so often around Pesach that one should think of oneself as having literally been there, having literally been brought out of Egypt, for we all have our “Narrow Places”. Those places and people and things in life that crimp and crush us down, where we make ourselves small even if we are not made small, that we may find our way through. For our ancestors, it was Egypt, and for us it might be a terrible relationship or some self-imposed hardship, or perhaps it is us realizing that we do not like who we were, not really, and when we cry, “This year, we are slaves, next year in liberation!”

But no. I was not sane, then. I was not grounded. I was not grounded for Pesach or Pesach Sheni, and now it is already Yom Kippur.

Ah well.

Perhaps it really is more fitting that I write this today, on this Day of Atonement.

I have looked back at the year before and seen who I was, and held that version of me up against the me who I feel I am now, and found her wanting.

I find myself still wanting.

I am pleased that I feel better about myself, now. I am pleased that I can see this re-opening of myself, and that I have found success, but I find myself still wanting for direction. I paw feebly for that which might be improvement, but I do not know what improvement even looks like. I still find myself riding the edge of overflow nigh constantly, and I wish for a path forward.

It is perhaps silly that it is only now, only a year some later, that I am first considering the fact that I am left as traumatized by this event as anyone. It was so easy to discount due to me not losing anyone directly in my life.

Here I am, though, nevertheless deeply changed by it all.
