systime (several days between Shavuot and Tisha B’Av)
-----------------------------------------------------

I have been thinking about what it is that has led to these last almost two weeks feeling so refreshing.

The larger part of it, I have decided, is that I have left Beth Tikvah. I am no longer surrounded by my books, by my office and apartment, by the same town that I have lived in for centuries now. I am no longer surrounded by the same people as always. I have not seen Rav in two weeks — though I have messaged her at least once a day to keep in touch; I do not wish to leave her in the dark, nor, I now realize, to be wholly without her presence — or Erin, or Shai, or Joseph. I have left behind my routine.

Instead, I am surrounded by strangers and crowds. Far less, now that the holiday is over, mind! I no longer feel quite so hemmed in by their presence, actual or perceived, for I feel the presence of others as a sort of weight on my shoulders. The streets are busy, as they must be with the other residents of this particular Jerusalem and the tour groups that come through during the day, but they are not so unbearably crowded.

Having left behind my routine, I have instead been nudged to help out with those tours. It is a task gladly taken up. I have done it in the past, of course, but also, it is a job that very quickly starts to feel like showing people around my home town. I have not lived here in some decades, but it remains as familiar as ever.

“From the beginning, the goal of this sim was to be historically accurate,” I said, voice raised above the noise of the city near the Pool of Siloam. I had been given charge of a group of visiting teachers and professors, with the understanding that I would be a good fit for them. I could see why; they all bore the marks of various hyperfixations, special interests, and other traits I see so often in myself.

Collecting a group like this is potentially dangerous, but everyone had been quite kind and well-behaved so far. There had been only one snippy comment from one scholar to another as we descended from the Essene gate to the pool.

I brushed my paws down over my skirt and apron — my trousers having been traded out for something a bit more accurate — and continued in my explanation. “Much of the initial investment was...ah, much of that energy was spent on accuracy, of course, yes? The works of our friend here, Doctor Nazaryan, for instance, gave us all the information we needed on the construction of Hezekiah’s tunnel, which brings water from...ah, from the Gihon spring to the Pool of Siloam, here.”

A slight man, who had been practically vibrating with anticipation leading up to our arrival at the pool, bobbed his head in acknowledgment. I could see the muscles standing out as cords in his arms, crossed tight against his front as though to bodily hold himself back from leaping in to water and wading up to the mouth of the tunnel to check our work.

I paused to let some of the group remove their sandals and step into the water to bathe their feet and, in one case, splash water up over themselves. It was not a very warm day, but neither had it been necessarily all that cool, and so I do not blame them for this.

Once we collected ourselves again and began the slow trek up along the stepped road, I continued speaking. “We continued then through the first few decades of...ah, of our planned recreation, beginning with the reign of Salome Alexandra. Our goal was to begin with the so-called ‘golden age’ of the Hasmonean period and continue to the fall of Jerusalem.”

“How did you handle historical personages?” asked an attendee.

I am unsure of their gender, Aaron-4-c Shaviro, and their pronouns in the perisystem architecture are the default-gray epicene that all new uploads start with. While they have not made public their upload date, this fact and a few other attributes about them and their behavior point to a relatively new upload — perhaps less than a decade.

However, their form was of a series of chaotically intersecting flat planes in an oil-sheen rainbow hue. The intersections of those ‘tiles’ appeared to form the outline of a standard bipedal humanoid form. In deference to the dress code of the sim — not strictly enforced at the moment, but certainly still encouraged — reflected within these tiles as though through a mirror were the shifting folds of linen that marked an approximation of period dress. It was quite distracting, but in a lovely way; I have seen many who eschew the strictly human form and many choices are lovely indeed — particularly their choice of ‘clothing’ — but the shifting chromatic aberations that washed over their tiles as they moved kept catching my eye.

It was a rejoicing in life sys-side. That is a thing I most certainly appreciate.

“Did someone,” they expanded, “for lack of a better term, _play_ Shelomtzion?”

I noded, offering a hint of a smile before returning my gaze to our slow progress up the stepped road, lest I stub a claw. “Yes, Professor Shaviro. Many of...ah, many of the core participants in the sim are actors and they often set forks to act out the appropriate roles, maintaining as much historical consistency as possible.”

“‘Historical consistency’?” I could hear the beginning of a sneer in their voice.

I resisted the urge to rise into defensiveness. “Also in the core are several historians, myself included — though...ah, though I will note that I am in no way credentialed as an historian — who provide scripts from which these historical personages may read, yes? Often, they appeared only during scheduled events. While the sim runs in full recreation mode for half the year, day-to-day recreation focuses on much smaller-scale stories.”

They walked in silence, and there was a suggestion of thoughtfulness about them. They did not have hands, nor did they have pockets, and yet there was a sense about them of them stuffing their hands in their pockets while they thought.

At last, they said, “I see. And this is why you deviated?”

Called back to the original lecture, I spoke up. “Ah. Right, yes. While we _began_ with the plan to follow this history, we quickly began to deviate, and for many reasons. There was...ah, rather, for instance, there was the question of what to do about wars, yes? We — both the core cast and the regular participants — found that we did not have the stomach for the gruesome. We did not wish to actually _play out_ violence against the poorer inhabitants of the city.”

“The curse of LARP,” another attendee, a lanky trans woman named Iosefina, said with a dramatic eyeroll, getting a laugh from our little crowd, including myself. We are no strangers to history and the role in which the less advantaged play.

“Indeed. We were...ah, we were not willing to run a milsim. We were unwilling to be cruel, or to have cruelty enacted upon us, yes?” I smiled as I looked out over the fronts of buildings lining the stepped way, and the other groups, docents, and actors that we came across. “And so we slowly let the plot fade. The progression of the years slowed to a stop, and now we are...ah, well, we are running our own stories in what is basically an early Herodian Jerusalem. It has become an alt history sim.”

The collection of facets that was Aaron-4-c made a noise that sounded rather like an amused snort, though I realized that, without the visual cues that accompany such, I had to work a little harder to understand such things. “I suppose that also means you have not needed to concern yourselves the Jesus problem.”

Responses from the group were largely pained amusement. Even Dr. Nazaryan, who had been peering back at the pool of Siloam and trying to glance out past the walls of the city — not easy from our current vantage — toward the spring it draws from managed a chuckle.

“Indeed. The purpose of our lives here is...ah, well, it is in many ways associated with Second Temple Judaism, but it is more associated with life in the Ancient Near East, yes? That we are doing so as Jews is...mm, it is a fact that falls out of the reality of the era; the Jews were a _people_ as much as — if not more than — they were members of a religion, yes?”

“Does that definition not still hold?” Aaron-4-c asked.

“Depends on if you’re talking to a Jew or not,” Iosefina said. Her laugh is a joy.

The team at the sim’s lobby — a sort of combination ticket office, orientation room, and lecture hall that exists separate from the sim itself — works to assign groups of visitors to a docent who will best fit them based on several factors before the tours ever begin, and I am pleased that they have gotten so good at their jobs over the years. I have heard tales from before I ever joined when groups were thrown together without much thought, back before much screening was done, and Messianic types would derail every tour for days on end. There are now several procedures in place to ensure that groups proceed smoothly through our town. There are two docents who are trained to engage comfortably with Christians and Messianic Jews ­— who are usually also Christian — who wish to tour our sim instead of one of the ones dedicated to their theology, and we are all trained with what to do if we are confronted with cases of Jerusalem Syndrome.

I am thankful for these things, but I am most thankful that they will consider the visitors’ knowledge, interests, and personalities when matching them with a docent.

This crowd with me was so easy to be with! Iosefina had a certain wit about her that I adored. It was the type of wit that is quick and sharp without being so quick as to make me feel slow or so sharp as to risk offense. Aaron-4-c was intriguing in their form and approach to identity, not to mention the formality of speech that echoes, in many ways, that of my own. Dr. Nazaryan had a depth of knowledge about the era that was astounding, and whenever the crowd invited him to infodump, he did so with joy, and came to a graceful stop each time without apologies.

When I mentioned that I wished to stay for the public season and that I was open to run tours — not something that I had done much in the past — the lobby team had interviewed me over the course of about four hours. They were careful to specify that, rather than judging my suitability, for my time in the sim over the decades had already proved such, they were creating a profile for this very reason. Thus, we spoke less about qualifications, and more about how I interacted with others.

We spoke about knowledge and my feelings on it. They spoke about the joy of hyperfixation. They spoke about the utility of autistic infodumping.

At one point early on, they asked me about social anxiety and the origin of my stammer, which does indeed get worse in situations where I am more anxious. I told them about the core of anxiety that drives me, and how I have come to channel it into a positive force within my life. I told them about the person I had dated about a century ago, who had told me that I spoke too much, used too many words, about how I learned to stop in the middle of speaking and judge how I should continue, and that is why it happens most often during conjunctions and explanations. I asked if they would like me to work on smoothing out my speech, and they got such a look of distress on their faces at the very idea that I knew that they would never ask this of me, and indeed, none of my tour members ever have, either. It is merely another fact that they consider when choosing the groups.

What a wonder it is to have friends! How easy it is to be kind! When I have sought out help in moving through the world, I have been granted it, yes, for I have a therapist whom I love and who loves me too, and I have already spoken before about the utility of diagnoses less as sentences coming down from on high and more as frameworks to help me move through the world, but also what joy! What joy that I have friends who are kind and who see these things as joyous parts of me, things without which I would be incomplete, and with which, I am all the more beautiful.

_Baruch atah, Adonai Eloheinu, melech ha’olam, she’asani b’tzelem Elohim;_ blessed are You, divine sovereign of the universe, who made me in the image of God– who, having stretched the measuring line when building the Earth’s foundations– who, having determined the season wild goats give birth– who, able to pull up Leviathan with a fishhook, could certainly understand the simple urges of an anxious, autistic woman in the years after the world ended, and know that sometimes, loving-kindness means merely being understood.

* * *

It is in the wee small hours of the morning and Jerusalem is quiet and it is mine.

I could not sleep, though this is no fault of Havivah’s. She is a quiet sleeper, and her bed is comfortable, and our conversation has been, after the first few days together, quite comforting rather than dire and full of pent-up sadness. We had not shared a bed before this month, but it is perhaps not so surprising that we fit well together. We are friends, and we both take comfort in physicality, and, most importantly, we are together in the moment. That last hurdle of actually being in the same place at the same time has been breached, and so we have shared our affections.

But even with this friendly coupling, I could not sleep, and so I have changed my shape, falling once more back to the form of a non-anthropomorphic skunk. This is not a critter that Ancient Judea ever knew. Skunks, like tomatoes and chilies and potatoes and corn, are a New World crop.

But it is before sunrise and the sim does not open for tours until mid-morning, and no one is awake aside from the guards, and so I feel free to trundle my way through the city. So long as I stay away from the Temple itself and any of the walls, I will be fine.

There is still within me the sense that there is a change occurring. I have mentioned it before, and I stand by what I said, that I feel I am unearthing some difference in myself and how I perceive the world. Bit by bit, I am coming to understand a bit more about what the Century Attack _actually_ meant to me. Of course it was impactful, but what that impact _is_ seems to be something that I left tucked away between the pages of some book or another, a forgotten ticket stub used to indicate something else, a pointer at anger, meaning lost until I opened up the book of my life and it fluttered to the ground and reminded me of its own inherent meaning.

That must have happened, that discovery of the ticket stub or slow unearthing of a cornerstone or whatever metaphor I am using in this moment of confusion, recently. This year, perhaps. The kennel of it was there during Yom HaShichzur, the potential was there, but it took perhaps that second moment of overflow riding so quickly on the heels of the first for me to sit up and think, “Oh, shit, I think this is something.”

These are the things that I think as I trundle through the city, wending my way through the empty stalls of the market, hunting down any bits of food left behind. I find no bread, as I had hoped, but I do find an apple that is mealy and bruised but not rotten. It takes only a few minutes in this form for me to find my tastes shifting, as though by being this low to the ground, having this poor of vision, feeling the sand grit beneath all four paws, it is a sort of mnemonic to trigger a broader shift within me in how I approach the world. I find little appealing about mealy apples, which in this case would have been cellared over the winter. Shaped as I am, though, it is a treat. It is easier to eat, even, easier to chew with this small mouth full of sharp teeth. I eat several bites before scampering away from the sound of a door opening nearby. All is well, I have received permission to be in this sim in this shape, but it is part of the mindset that I be skittish.

Down the alley I have darted, just a few feet away from the market, I find a jug of olives that is covered by a lid of woven rushes, and I push that aside to steal and eat three or four olives from within. Luxury!

Some decades back, I am not sure how long ago without checking, I spoke with another member of our clade, And The End Of Memory Lies Beneath The Roots, about what it would take to look like a critter such as this. I had spoken with Roots on a few occasions previously, tuning my shape with their assistance. They are a sensewright and a sensorium artist of some renown, and they have often lent their talents to those who want to know how best to experience a particular way of being. In my case, I saw them to better understand how it would feel to have a facial structure more akin to that of an actual skunk, and then to understand how it would feel to have the whispier, thinner fur on my tail such as a real skunk might have, as opposed to the more pillowy tails that many furries with skunk presentations might have.

At last, I saw them to work out how to just present as a skunk. The actual, housecat-sized animal, rather than this anthropomorphized identity that so many of us within the clade have. We worked together for a few weeks to first make it comfortable for me to walk on all fours, for me to be small, and then to tune my shape, my locomotion, and then from there to work on my senses, tuning up my sense of smell and down my vision. At last, we worked on habits, building up behaviors that came naturally and which made me feel yet more like a skunk.

Thus me now, just as I lived for more than a year after that training, trundling around Jerusalem, eating dropped apples and filching olives from a jar. They are briny and the salt hurts my mouth, and they are fatty and unctuous, and I imagine they may well make me sick if I do not follow them up with water.

I start the process of trundling down the stepped way to the pool where I may drink my fill, and marvel at the shift in perspective.

I have lived here for years, if you take all of the time spent here in three-month stints. I know this Jerusalem well. I know the people here, and the buildings. I know the scent of the air before dawn, and in the heat of the day. I know the small sounds of the night, and the voices.

But always, I have been here as What Right Have I — or Simcha, as my character is named — and never as this critter. Never have I been here as a nameless thing, an animal without the appearance of humanity.

When I spent that year and change as a skunk shaped like this, the biggest worry that I heaped onto myself was whether I had somehow stepped away from that which made me _b’tzelem Elohim,_ as though by having four legs instead of two, I might suddenly be no longer holy and alive. It was a sense of guilt, as though I was eschewing some God-given identity, rather than leaning harder into an identity that had been core to me for centuries.

It was silly.

More than a century ago, I spoke with Roots again in their role as sensewright in order to see what it would take for me to have a male body. They did not ask why, and I did not say. I did not say because I knew that I could not boil those feelings down into an elevator pitch, something that I could offer at the drop of the hat that would give enough clarity to satisfy both my interlocutor and my own desire to be honest.

I wanted, I think, to come at femininity the long way around. I wanted to approach it from the outside. We as a clade are still a majority of us queer women. We were born into femininity and, although we have toyed with it and made it distinctly ours, we love — and loved — it.

But I wanted to experience _becoming._ I wanted to experience the joy of finding a home in my body. I wanted to partake in the act of creation with myself as the work being created.

I spent the next five years then transitioning back towards femininity. It was not that I did not enjoy masculinity in its own way, but it was not me. I was not that. I was this. Following the lead of a group I found via the feeds, I took hormone injections (which were not wholly ineffectual, sys-side, as they did apply some faint changes to my sensorium and other systems in my body, even if they did not apply physiological changes themselves) and would modify my shape incrementally, forking in small changes as I went.

I found myself not _quite_ back where I started, but still fairly close. It was a joy, in a way, to realize that the life I had been living before was essentially full of the joy that I found in returning, that we were already happy as we were, many of us.

And so of course it was silly that I have these worries about the shape of my body and whether that made me in the image of God. It was silly even for someone who had not already had such a conscious hand in their own making, but for me, doubly so. I said so many times that it became something of a joke that HaShem made grapes but not wine and wheat but not bread, and that They made me as I was, not as I am and certainly not as I was becoming. I was born Michelle Rachel Hadje, not What Right Have I of the Ode clade.

And so I laugh now as I trot down the stairs to the edge of the Pool of Siloam and lower my head to drink from the cool, fresh water, lapping, and enjoying the way it soothes my four paws.

Today, Rav as sent a fork to come visit me in the sim and spend the evening with Havivah and I. It is Shabbat, and for this, the town is quiet in a way that it has not been all week. The sim, after all, closes on Shabbat, with the last guests being admitted at noon and the sim being swept automatically at sundown of all who have not been given approval. We are even allowed to progress small plots forward on Shabbat itself, though nothing consequential in the off season.

We sit up on the roof, then, and sing our blessings quietly — confidently, but quietly so as not to disturb the peace of the night — before we dine on bread grilled over the low fire, and small cubes of lamb, spiced well, and some rough hummus that one of Havivah’s neighbors made. They, too, are dining on their roof, and while conversation will occasionally flow between our two groups, we seem more comfortable to enjoy a pleasant level of separation afforded by the night air.

As the night proceeds and the fire burns lower, so too do our voices grow quieter, and we tell each other stories of our lives gone by.

“Years and years back,” Havivah says, “I worked as an organizer. Sounds so silly, saying now. An organizer! What do you do? Why, I organize! And what do you organize? People! You sort them into boxes? Organize by height? By weight?” Even her cackles are quieter.

“But no, I tell people what to do. I organize them into teams and tell them where to go and what to accomplish. I was good at it, too, because I made it feel like it was all their idea. I talk and talk and talk, and I write and write and write, and I learn what it is that people are trying to get done, and if it’s something I believe too, something I really want to see happen — because you know you can’t organize for something you don’t believe in! — then I try and make that thing happen. I bring after the war the people back home who want to come home, or I help set up a protest, or I work on setting up sims on the ‘net to facilitate the right people meeting. Facilitate! That must’ve been the word I used the most. Havivah the facilitator! Ha! Only I was called Anita back then.”

Rav and I chuckle. It feels good to have her here, and she and I have been leaning against each other nigh constantly through the night.

“I remember...ah, I remember some skills like that from our last few years, phys-side,” I say. “Though also... mm, well, there was still some of it in being a rabbi.”

Rav laughs. “Perhaps not quite as much trying to get people to do things and then have them think that it was their own idea. Sometimes with committee chairs at the shul, though.”

“Not with the ANRC?” Havivah asks.

Rav wrinkles her nose. “There is a reason I am not the chair.”

I glance over to her, head tilted, and ask, “Did you not like it the times that you were? I know that...ah, that you refused to continue on to another term the two times you were.”

She shrugs, shoulder brushing against my own. “I do not feel like I did a bad job, but neither do I feel like it was the role I was meant to take. It is a role more for people like Erin, or True Name. Havivah, you would do splendid in the role, from what you have said. That trick of making people think it was their own idea? That would go over quite well when getting all the committees to point in the same direction.”

Havivah snorts. “Ohhh, no. Don’t you dare, From Whence! I’ll not be roped into working again.”

My down-tree smiles sweetly over to her. “I am merely saying that there is a role that is all but built for you, my dear. A bit of training on the specifics, and you could step right into it.”

“Ay! Training!” she cries, throwing up her hands, and there is laughter from the couple on the next roof over. “Simcha, rein in your down-tree! Next thing, she will have me shaking babies and kissing hands!”

Rav and I both laugh, and my cocladist shakes her head. “No, no. I would never push anyone into that! We will leave that for the Old Diplomat’s friends, yes?”

_“Zichronah livracha,”_ Havivah says, quieter and with a calmer smile on her face. “What Right Have I has told me that you miss her every day.”

Rav smiles, dips her snout, and lifts her tumbler of wine in acknowledgment. “I do, yes, but the world as moved on.”

Understanding this to be a request for the conversation to move on as well, I smile over to Havivah. “Did you go to school for your work in organizing?”

“Oh, yes! I saved up every penny. Public policy major in undergrad, then a master’s in–” She sits up straighter and lifts her nose into the air haughtily. “–organizational leadership and innovation.” Then she is back to cackling.

“See? Then you already have plenty of training,” Rav says, then laughs at the groan this gets. “That does sound like an interesting enough program, though. Did you enjoy your time?”

“Absolutely. It was meaningful to me. It was important! Even the legal side, I’d get all wrapped up in those regulations and learn them front to back, and then find all the crazy little ways around them to get done what we wanted. We’d run things smooth and when everything was ticking along, it felt like momentum. _Political_ momentum, like we were getting the world to tick along with us. School, it was school, but it was the topic that interested me, and I liked that.” She downs the last of her wine and sets the goblet aside. “Feh, Havivah the chatterbox! You get me going. Come, tell me of you two! What schooling did you do?”

Rav casts a sidelong glance my way and smirks. I hold up my paws in surrender, and the three of us laugh.

“When...ah, rather, before we uploaded, we got a bachelor’s in theater education,” I start. “We were a theater teacher, back phys-side, yes? We taught high school students and put on plays a few times a year, and then...ah, and then acted in community theater in the summer.”

Havivah shakes her head. “This I knew, but still I can’t picture it. I can’t picture you up on stage, bossing around a bunch of children, and only in some things can I picture you acting.”

“I was...ah, well, we were much different back then, yes?” I say with a hint of a shrug. “To see more what we were like back then, you would have to meet perhaps the fifth stanza, yes? They are the ones who have stuck with theater. They have...ah, they have a troupe all of their own — though it has long since grown to include far more than themselves.”

“Do not discount it too much,” Rav says gently, then casts a wink to Havivah. “She has performed a few times over the years. She even narrated Bernstein’s “Kaddish” at one point, and to great acclaim.”

Havivah looks on me with a new sense of awe that I have not seen in her before. “You did?”

The wine is warm within me, and these are my friends, and I feel comfortable, here in this place that is a sort of home to me, with the stars above and the fire burning low so that I am only a shadow to the world around, and so there is no cause for me to blush or play coy, so I simply nod. “I did, yes. Twice, even, though...ah, though the first time was to a much smaller audience. It was that which won me the second performance. Both were a delight, but...ah, but just as I have stepped away from pastoral and congregational duties, I have largely stepped away from theater.”

“And From Whence kept all of those?”

My cocladist nods. “The third stanza got our bachelor of religious studies as Oh, But To Whom–”

“It was...ah, it was after we uploaded, after the text line was well established and books were being uploaded, that we wound up getting that — a focus on...ah, on Jewish studies — through a correspondence course, yes,” I explain.

“–and then I got my semicha, the first offered sys-side, one of the first to be ordained as a rabbi, here.”

“And that is a degree?” Havivah asks.

“Yes, it is technically a master’s in divinity.”

“Ay! Surrounded by scholars!”

Rav snorts. “Scholar, singular. That title goes to What Right Have I far more than me.”

“Yes, but a rabbi! Three degrees!”

“She has seven.”

Once more, Havivah looks to me with awe, and this time, I do blush.

“I...ah, well, yes. BA in theater education, BRS in Jewish studies, BA in writing, a BRS in comparative religion, an MA in theopoetics and writing, the MDiv we mentioned... ah, and a ThD in contemporary rabbinics, yes.”

We sit in silence, with only the soft sounds of the fire and the small sounds of the night, until at last one of the neighbors lets out a low whistle, and Havivah follows it up with, _“Shkoyach!”_

We all fall to laughing.

I have found myself at several points since reading _The Sign of Jonas_ wondering if I should include the events of the day into this journal, and in some cases I have and in some I have not. Each time I find myself wondering at it, I either start recording, or I second-guess myself and find that I have to drop the topic lest I freeze up in the moment.

There are times when I think that a topic will be interesting, or a sight, or a thought, and I will find that it simply does not hold up when set down in words like this, and I have abandoned several entries that I have drafted on second reading, finding them boring, awkward, ill-suited for reading.

This one, though, as with many of the ones I have kept, I leave in because for some reason, it is just a moment that is poignant enough to be a comforting read after the fact — at least, to me — and also because it carries some kernel of import with it.

In this case, I find myself wondering at all of the schooling that I have gone through. there is so much joy in it! I have seven times before gone through the process of applying to a school, waiting with anticipation to hear whether or not I was accepted, had that moment of celebration, and then set to work on learning. In the case of my graduate programs, I have also written some of the things I am most proud of.

All that schooling, and yet here I am, enjoying a simple night of food and quiet and company from those I have come to treasure. I treasure all of those lovely classes, learning and learning and learning, and I treasure this night, too.

It occurs to me that some of what I really treasure most in my life is just the fact that I have _time._ I have time to study. I have time to write. I have time to get upset or to be happy or to fall into and out of love! I have time for so much, here, and, even though there was this interruption, one that affected us all in more ways than we could ever have guessed, there is still limitless time ahead of me to work to better the world, it feels. Will my end be a minute away? An hour? Will I even know when it is coming? History has said no, and also it has given me more than three centuries. All there is to do is the work of the repair of the world, ends be damned.

And so yes, this conversation is a little nothing. It is a little recounting of the past, a little reveling in the present; a little bettering of the self and a little resting on the sabbath; and these, too, are Torah.
