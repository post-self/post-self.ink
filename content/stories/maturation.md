When Melody had first uploaded she'd expected a certain amount of patronizing attitude. She was only twenty years old when she'd made the decision to move to Lagrange, and by the time she arrived in early 2302 there were more than a trillion instances extant. Young twice over, every time someone offered unsolicited advice or bemused commentary simply because of her newness, she would internalize her reactionary burst of resentment, put on a smile, and go about her day.

What she hadn't expected is that a decade later the only thing to change would be her patience for it wearing thin.

The demographics of the System didn't move like back phys-side. There was no steady churn of aging and death, no automatic promotion to some new rung in society by virtue of existing long enough. Here, with rare exception, everyone simply continued, and ten years in Melody found herself still feeling uncomfortably new and being treated as such.

Part of it, of course, was her chosen appearance. Plenty of people on the System spent their early efforts on refining how they looked, in ways both subtle and drastic. It hadn't taken long to grow accustomed to aliens, fantasy creatures, robots, anthropomorphized animals, and even more unusual scattered through a population still predominantly human.

Melody, meanwhile, looked exactly as she had when she'd arrived, in all her youth. This fact came up from time to time among those she was closest to, and especially those who'd effected radical change in themselves.

"I cannot believe after all these years you're still the same, plain girl," said the silver dragon, teasing her as he draped a wing around her shoulders.

Clemens' words earned him a gentle elbow in the side in response before she nestled into the warmth of the leathery limb. The two of them were bench sitting in a wintry sim, and she'd tuned how much of the cold she could feel up for sake of enjoying how her companion's body heat was able to fend it off on her behalf.

"It isn't my fault I'm the only person who has existed, ever, without a longing to be anything besides myself," she playfully griped back. "No dysphoria. No dreams of a tail, or fins, or metal skin. Even the birth mark, that's me."

The wine-colored oval, an inch in diameter, sat squarely atop her right collarbone, peeking out past the neckline on many of the blouses and dresses she favored. It had always seemed, to her, a pleasant offset to a soft chin, pert lips, dark brown eyes, and the light brown hair she styled into a bob. Standard features, forgettable even, but the only ones that felt right to her when she looked in the mirror.

"I go back and forth on whether that is a curse or a blessing," he rumbled in response. The shape of his wedge snout and long, thin tongue gave every ‘s’ a sibilance. "Perhaps if we were still stuck back phys-side, I would say that you're lucky. Here, though? Unlike so many of us, you have never had to struggle to reconcile form and identity, nor does playing with such seem to appeal."

She felt him nuzzle the top of her head, then a snuffle, followed by a warm exhalation. There was a physical intimacy between the two of them by now, but one that didn't stray toward sensuality. This suited her fine; even before uploading her interest in such had been confused and limited.

"There is joy in the striving and experimentation," Clemens continued, sounding insistent, "and in the becoming, even if it isn't a final state. I worry sometimes you deny yourself something essential of this place."

Melody felt her cheeks flush warm, and for the umpteenth time in her comparatively short life, she suppressed a cutting reply. She liked the dragon, but the conversation was turning into more of a lecture than was wanted. She came back with a lie instead.

"I changed my name, didn't I? Surely that has to count for something."

It was the best kind of lie, having a kernel of truth. Her birth name had been _Carla_ of all things, but long before she'd let them core out her head and scan the pieces, or whatever uploading was, she'd had people calling her Melody. Even her parents.

The wing around her tightened briefly, a friendly squeeze. Maybe Clemens had realized he'd touched a nerve, even if he didn't quite understand how.

"I am simply doing as a good friend ought and occasionally poking at the parts of you that seem most set in stone," he insisted. "You are more than free to do the same to me."

"So I should ask you if you've found your way back to being on speaking terms with your downtree?" the girl countered, sounding peeved. "I only bring it up because _that's_ been a constant all the years I've known you and–"

"Enough! Please," he interrupted, sounding pained. "The betrayal...the situation is not nearly as simple as you think it is. It isn't just like a falling out between friends. This person, they're like a sibling, but also a parent, but they're also you, and it's just so hard to explain to–"

"To someone singular." Melody took her turn cutting in. Triggering a reaction had soothed her own, enough that she was able to muster being solicitous. "C'mon, let's talk about nicer stuff. I'm not gonna be here all that much longer."

The dragon accepted her olive branch, and the conversation trended to smaller, safer topics while he kept her warm, and beneath the cloak of his wing she used fingertips to pet over the pebbling of his scales. Before too long an internal alarm nudged her, so she shrugged out into the chill, then stood.

"And when will I see you next?" he asked. The grin on his face suggested he knew the answer, but there was important ritual in the asking.

She grinned back. "You'll see me when you see me," came the reply, response to a call. Then she was gone, stepping between sims to elsewhere.

-----

From snow-bound hill to pastoral field, then from there to sweltering seaside town and beyond. Melody lingered a predetermined amount of time in each, following an inner itinerary only she was privy to. Her devotion to her self-assigned schedule was slavish, and over the years she'd entertained plenty of inquiries as to why she did as she did.

"It's the chance of it all, the novelty," she once explained to another regular, perched on a stool in the tavern sim she favored Saturday nights before bed. The aesthetic was a pastiche of a thousand trashy fantasy stories, driving past tacky imitation into playful satire.

"I could run into someone I see all the time, or someone I haven't seen in ages. Maybe there's nobody, and I get to meet new people, or I'm given the chance to explore or to get lost in my own thoughts. Hell, that happens enough times maybe I go poke around a new sim instead. Not like there's a shortage."

"You shound like a gambler," came half-slurred commentary in response, "like you care more about a dopamine hit than anything elsh." Easy enough to tell they were looking to get a rise out of her.

"You might be right about that," she said, choosing to lean into the critique, "but we're all responsible for our own happiness here. If I find mine in metaphorically tearing open a dozen packages a day to see who or what's inside, what's the big deal?"

The grunt she got back signaled further discussion was unwanted, and that suited her just fine. There were other motivations at play behind her routine, ones she only felt as echoes of anxiety, or half-glimpsed in periods of deep introspection. Ones she'd rather someone not blurt out, thus forcing her to actually reckon with them.

-----

Melody was trying out an urban park sim someone had recommended to her the day she first met the old man.

Having spent the better part of an hour wandering a paved path around a lake without running into anyone else, the urge to move on was just about impossible to ignore. Before she could, in the distance, she spotted faint movement and curiosity kept her pacing along.

Nearing, she could make out a flock of ducks waddling and flapping next to the water, all of them clustered around a weathered bench. On the bench sat a man and, as she drew closer, a shaky toss of one arm launched a spray of what she imagined was food of some kind. The water fowl scurried and pecked after it in response.

"Hello," she called from far enough away to avoid startling him. With no immediate response she approached and tried again, louder this time. "Hello?"

The face that turned her way was lined and creased with age, more so than she could remember ever having seen sys-side. Watery, pale brown eyes squinted as if trying to make her out, and it was obvious when they did because his expression brightened immediately. The kind smile she got was half obscured by a steel gray, bushy mustache that matched his brows, and Melody found herself immediately charmed.

"Well, hey there, sweetheart," he greeted her, "would you like to come sit down?" His voice was a low tenor, warm and rough, and she was immediately put in mind of the resonant creaking wood could make when flexed. "I've got more corn if you want to feed the ducks with me."

"I mean, sure, why not?" she answered and moved to join him, only to get redirected from his left side to his right.

"Better go thattaway, hon, that ear is my good one. Don't want you to have to shout."

It was just unexpected enough to throw Melody's usually capable social skills into disarray. More signs of how aged the man was were noted as she settled herself, and rather than asking his name or offering her own, what came out of her mouth was a baffled, "you're _old_."

He immediately laughed. "Boy, nothing gets by you, does it?"

She could feel herself redden, but decided explanation beat apology.

"I just can't remember ever seeing someone actually old before. Like, there's plenty of people who look a little elderly, but it's always been surface level. No one has had a bad ear or a tremor or, I don't know, a limp, you know?"

There was a nod in response. "People like to toss on a veneer of experience. Think it gets 'em taken more seriously," he said, then looked her way. "It does, sometimes. Bet you get talked down to a lot for similar reasons."

She grimaced and decided to sneak in the introduction she'd missed earlier. "My name's Melody Foster."

"Abraham Todd of the Todd clade," he answered before continuing on the subject. "Rare to see anyone disadvantaged, but it happens. Sometimes there's a hiccup between an upload and the System the techs can't sort out. A lot of the time it's about identity, though. Folks upload having been a way their whole lives and don't want to give up a thing that's been a part of them for so long, even if it's a challenge."

The man moved a rumpled paper bag between the two of them and Melody reached in to grab and toss her own offering to the ducks.

"Is that what this is, then," she asked, "all of this is wrapped up in who you are?"

Abraham shook his head and palmed more corn, doling out individual kernels toward selected targets, like a king showing favor to his subjects.

"This is something else. If you wanted to be charitable, you could call it experimentation, though I'll just as readily accept 'playing pretend'." When all he got in response was a baffled look, he chuckled. "How old are you, sweetheart?"

"Twenty plus ten." Not everyone split their age pre and post upload, but somehow it sounded better than just 'thirty' to her.

"Ah, so you're _new_ new," he replied, not unkindly. "Well, I'm fifty-two plus one-hundred-and-thirty three. Forgive me a short lecture? I suspect you get them unasked for often enough, but it's necessary to answer your question."

Melody found it easy to nod in response. Having permission asked up front did a lot to make the idea palatable.

"Something that will become clear to you, eventually, is after a certain point of continued existence, you're going to start to go a little mad. I'm sure you've heard the stories about how strange older clades and uploads can get—hell, you were here when _On the Perils of Memory_ was published. You must have read it, I feel like nearly everyone did."

She nodded again. Interrupting with actual words seemed contrary to the spirit of a lecture.

"It looks a little bit different for everyone. Your clade ends up in a civil war over some slight from five decades ago, or you hyper-fixate on a hobby until your existence is nothing but that, or you completely isolate yourself. As many flavors as there are lives here, you get the idea. You spend however long you can manage indulging all those passions and wants free from restraint but, in the end, no matter how much joy you find, there will come a day life tastes a little less sweet than the one before.

"We're just not built for an endless, abundant eternity. Doesn't matter if you were uploaded two hundred years ago or yesterday, we're all still stuck with a brain that is accustomed to scarcity, danger, and death."

"So what do you–" the girl blurted out before stopping herself at having inadvertently interrupted, only for him to encourage her to continue. "What do you do about it? I mean, you don't seem, uh, crazy."

"Oh, I very much am," Abraham said, then chuckled, "but maybe not quite as much as I should be. You need to find a purpose to shape yourself around. A theory, or a project, a life's work scale of endeavor. Lets you stave it off some, and channel it into something _you_ choose, instead of it choosing for you, which brings us back around to your question."

Melody squinted at the old man and then gave the ducks another toss from the bag, watching them trundle around in pursuit, sometimes overtop of one another.

"Alright, so this is what you're pouring yourself into, then? Your great work?"

He nodded, then added clarification. "One part of it. My whole clade has taken an interest in all of the things we've lost on account of uploading. Like how nobody can forget, or how there are no children here. How nobody gets sick anymore." One wrinkled hand gestured over his form. "In my case, the fact that no one here ages, not like people do phys-side."

"Well, you seem to be doing it very earnestly and authentically," she observed with an approving tone. "Calling it 'pretend' seems harsh."

The laugh that Abraham barked in response startled her. "Oh sweetheart," he replied, "what do you think we're doing right now?" He pointed at the ducks, beginning to disperse in the absence of more to eat. "These aren't real. The corn isn't real. Nothing alive is being sustained, and they aren't feeling pleased and happy at treats or full bellies." The man shrugged and smiled, a touch sadly. "We do it anyway, for our own sakes, our own enjoyment, but that's all it is."

Melody felt an odd surge of anxiety, frowned, and almost without realizing it was back up on her feet. "I...I need to go," she stated, looking shaken, "thanks for the talk."

She waited only as long as it took to get an acknowledging, understanding nod from Abraham before she left the sim, but not soon enough to miss his parting words.

"That's all any of this is."

-----

Melody spent the next few days throwing herself into her rhythms and routines. She visited her favorite spots at her usual times, delighting in the company she found there. Ate good food, listened to music she loved, slept in on Saturday, and, in general, should have been having a very good time.

Through it all, she was accompanied by a small, doubting voice in her head questioning the meaning, purpose, and reality of things. Sometimes of herself, too.

There's no good age to have an existential crisis but, if one must, being young helps. In Melody's case that meant only a few days of dwelling before anxiety transformed into frustration, and soon after that into anger. How dare Abraham fill her with unfounded doubt? No matter what he'd implied, she knew what she was experiencing felt real, and that was justification enough for it to be so, and to be of value.

It was in this fit of pique she began trawling the urban park sim again, storming toward the familiar figure sat on a bench when she spied him on her third day of attempts. As before, he was holding court for the ducks. His smile of recognition faded into puzzlement at her expression.

"Hey!" she yelled. "What the hell is your problem?"

He blinked, and there was a pregnant pause before he replied in a mild tone. "I don't think I have one, sweetheart, but you sure seem to."

A decade of suppressing her own irritation for sake of being polite was serving her poorly in maintaining a head of steam. With Abraham's own response being polite confusion, she felt all the heightened emotion that had driven her here draining away.

"You said everything was fake," she said, reduced to sounding sullen. "You said nothing was real."

"Sounds a touch black and white for something I'd say, but nevermind that, you're upset. Please, come sit down, let's talk it through." He had the same bag of corn as last time, and offered it to her. "Feed the ducks?"

"You mean _play pretend_?" she griped back at him as she sat, and he made a noise of comprehension.

"Didn't like that idea, eh? Well, to set the record straight, I think that the System is real, that we're real, along with all of the things that we feel and think and hope and want. Once you get past that, well, it gets fuzzy."

Melody huffed. "Define fuzzy," she demanded.

"Alright, alright," he said with a slight smile, "it's a little bit like stage magic. You know what that is?"

He waited until she nodded before continuing.

"So you go to a magic show knowing full well that magic isn't real. You know it's sleight of hand, misdirection, props, memory games. Maybe you even know how some of it works. When you're there, though, what you _want_ is to be tricked. You want to see something you can't explain, that makes it feel real, even if it isn't.

"Now hold that in your head, and think about this: You've got no physical body that needs energy from food, and you can tune your own drives down. You don't need to be hungry, and you don't need to eat. When you do, the act is entirely simulated, the System interpreting and passing your consciousness a thousand thousand sensations. The tastes, textures, that feeling of satiety."

"I _like_ eating," Melody interjected.

"Of course you do," the man replied, "and I do too. I even like being hungry sometimes. Makes things taste better, and it's a handy internal clock. My point is, they're the same. We do these things knowing where we exist is a sort of shared hallucination, that it's just code, but we want it to feel real. We want to be tricked, because that feels good when you're in on it too."

There was another huff from her. "What's the point of that? The knowing part, I mean. I feel like it just takes away from the enjoyment."

"You know you don't have to listen to me, right?" Abraham pointed out, pitching more corn onto the grass. For a moment the conversation quieted, the only commentary quacks from the milling birds.

"I think it's important to be mindful of where and what we are," he continued when she said nothing. "Isn't it kind of wonderful, though? How spectacular it is that it can feel so real? It's a comfort, too, for someone doing what I'm doing. Imagine how frightening it would be to lose your hearing, your vision, your balance through physical failure. This feels real, but I know it's artificial."

The girl picked a single kernel from the bag and tossed it, bouncing it off the back of one of the ducks. It didn't seem to mind.

"How's that work, anyway? The getting old thing," she asked.

"Well, I—aw, I'm sorry sweetheart, but I've got somewhere I need to be. Could we meet up over tea later today? I know a great, little shop. It's got all these frogs."

"I don't _do_ plans." Melody's tone was flat.

Abraham's brow furrowed a moment, but he didn't push back. "That's fine, then. You know you can find me here sometimes. I suppose we'll just leave it up to chance," he said, then groaned as he slowly stood. "Stay and feed 'em a while. Nothing wrong with a bit of play, after all, is there?"

He vanished before she could reply either way, leaving her to mull his words and, eventually, do as he suggested.

-----

It took more than week before she caught up with Abraham again. Some of that had been her delaying, uncertain if she even wanted to talk to him again. Eventually curiosity won out, and she made a habit of wandering past what she was coming to think of as 'his' bench a couple times a day.

There was a dull surprise at how her heart fluttered when she finally found him sitting there.

Picking up the topic from their last conversation, they dug in together on just how Abraham's aging project functioned, her questions providing a scaffold for him to build off of. It seemed that his downtree was quite the talented sensorium artist, and every couple days the two of them would duplex so he could work very gradual changes into both form and function.

"You'd think knowing what I was doing I'd be ready for it out and about," he said, then shook his head and chuckled. "It's amazing what you don't notice, until you suddenly do. We tweaked the hearing on my left side down four times, and I'd have sworn I was fine. That fifth time, though, all of a sudden it was so noticeable.

"Of course, it's amazing what you get used to, too. That was years ago, and I don't even really think about it now. Just nudge folks to talk into my good ear, or instinctively tilt my head that way."

There were other tricks, too, as Melody discovered when she caught Abraham misremembering something from one of their previous meetings. He was visibly proud when he explained at the end of every day he randomly picked three or four specific memories to tuck in an exo, mimicking a mind that wouldn't quite hold onto everything it experienced.

"Sure, I know I'm missing something, but I just guess. Sometimes I even get it right, ha!"

She almost didn't notice when he steered the conversation into asking about her, and the top item of his agenda seemed to be prying into the reason it'd taken so long for the two of them to meet again.

"Tell me about this 'no plans' business," he requested. "Not like I haven't been turned down before, but never for a reason like that. And I'm old!" He laughed, and she smiled despite herself.

"I suppose I'm particular about how I promise my time," she said, attempting to keep her answer light and breezy. "I don't want to disappoint someone else by cancelling at the last minute. Besides, leaving things to chance makes for a lot of fun surprises."

Abraham was shaking his head even before she finished speaking. "Nope, I don't think that's it at all, or at least not completely." His immediate pushback took her off guard, and he pressed forward into the resulting silence. "You pulled back from the idea like you'd touched a hot stove. That must come from a place of anxiety. Maybe you're the one that doesn't want to be disappointed?"

"I'm just—I'm managing my expectations," Melody replied defensively.

He offered a sympathetic look. "How's that working out for you, then? Must make it hard to have friends."

"I have plenty of acquaintances, including you, so it's working just fine. Can we _please_ talk about something else?"

The briefest hesitation before her companion nodded suggested an interest in continuing to poke at the idea, then making the choice not to for her sake. Instead they found some safer common ground discussing different sim designs and parted shortly thereafter.

This time Melody was left dwelling on her own words, rather than his. She was fine, wasn't she?

-----

"Clemens! I haven't seen you in weeks."

The dragon had clearly been lost in thought based on the way her greeting caused him to jerk his head up in surprise. When he saw who it was his snout split into a weary smile.

"Dear girl, it has been a while, hasn't it? Come, warm yourself for a few minutes before I must be off again." A wing stretched out invitingly, and Melody didn't hesitate to wrap herself up in it, sheltering from the cold and snow in their usual meeting place.

"You're leaving?" She tried to keep the disappointment out of her voice, but he'd known her too long to not hear it.

"I am sorry, it has been a tough time of late." His nonspecific words earned him a gentle elbow in the ribs, so he added, "the clade has been...disturbed. Our downtree has made contact."

Melody still had no idea what the point of contention was between him and his pseudo-progenitor, nor the relationship between him and his co-cladists. Only that as long a she'd known him it was a sore spot, one she'd pushed on to manage their conversations away from sensitive subjects. A sudden wish for having treated it any other way had her pursuing the subject.

"Can you tell me a little bit about what's going on?" she inquired.

The long pause in response to her words stretched from consideration into discomfort. His wing slipping from around her shoulders as he took a step away was not the answer she'd expected. Looking up at his face, for the first time in ages, she couldn't tell what expression was painted on it.

"I'm sorry," he repeated, then vanished from the sim, leaving her standing and shivering in the cold. She stayed there brooding over the unsatisfying encounter until her exposed skin prickled toward numbness, then disappeared herself.

-----

The lack of Clemens being in the usual spot didn't stop her from checking in, but as weeks passed without the dragon's presence, looking came with a side helping of worry she didn't like. Abraham was proving to be a reliable fill-in, at least, the old man present more often than not when she poked her nose into the park sim.

"So what do you plan to do with, you know, all of this?" she asked him one day. Inquiring after the aging project was always a reliable way to nudge him off talking about her. Melody had been complaining of Clemens' absence, and he'd immediately used it as a springboard to asking about her attachment style again.

"Enh? What do you mean?" he replied, a touch befuddled.

"When you explained it to me before," she elaborated, "you made it sound like there was some sort of goal you had. Your whole clade is focused on consequences from uploading, right? I can't imagine you'd do this for years and years without something planned. Unless you're just having fun."

"Fun, ha! You try being old, see how fun it is," he rasped out with a chuckle, then tilted his head and looked thoughtful. "That's part of it, though, acting as inspiration for others to attempt this or something similar. Build a little more perspective in a small part of the System, if we're lucky. Probably a book, maybe some interviews with folks once word gets out. That's all besides the active impact of it."

It was Melody's turn to be confused. "What do you mean by that?"

He waved vaguely in her direction. "Mean _you_ by that. You and everyone else I'm on good terms with. Can't help but influence each other by virtue of being in contact, you know? Hell, just by bumping into me you've probably thought more about the idea of aging than most folks around here ever have. Bet you've got feelings about what it's been like dealing with me doing this as we've chatted."

"Well, I do, do you know how annoying–"

Abraham held further words at bay with a lifted hand. "Save it for now. I'll interview you about it eventually, I imagine. If we ever get past random bench buddies, anyways."

She rolled her eyes in response. "Save it yourself, _old man_. You try and get into it with me again I'll start throwing corn at _you_." A huff followed, not a serious one, and she stood. "I'm gonna go look for the dragon again."

The man nodded amiably. "I'll probably be here a while yet if you get bored," he said, then gave her a look, brow furrowed. "This Clemens, he's very dear to you, isn't he?"

Conflicting emotions wrote their way across Melody's face before she finally answered. "He's the person that I've known the longest." It was the most she was willing to acknowledge, and when Abraham offered no remark in return, she vanished, heading for a different bench in a far colder sim.

-----

Once again, no rendezvous with the silver dragon was on offer, but there was something different. On the bench seat, untouched by the drifting snow and gusts of wind was an envelope with her name scrawled on the outside.

Curious, she retrieved it and tore it open. The missive inside took only minutes to read, but the effect on the girl was profound. Confusion into shock, then into the sort of grief that hits like a punch to the gut. She collapsed to her knees, gasping, unseeing, and then the first ragged sob tore its way out of her.

-----

Abraham startled as no more than ten minutes after leaving, Melody re-entered the sim, crying uncontrollably and lurching forward to collapse against the bench.

"Oh no! Sweetheart, what happened?" he asked, alarmed, hesitating before reaching over to pat her shoulder tentatively.

It took several attempts for her to find the way to words. "He's GONE," she managed, and even admitting that fact sent her back into tears, ones that wouldn't stop, not for all the attempts at comforting her the old man made.

Holding her with one arm while she wept against his shoulder, he eventually noticed the crumpled, clutched page in Melody's grasp. Awkwardly teasing it free with his other hand, he smoothed it out on his knee before lifting it up to read.

> My dear girl,
> 
> I had hoped to do this in person, but life seems to have dictated our paths shall not cross, and I wanted to respect your position on direct communication. My time grows short, and so this note must suffice for explanation.
> 
> A portion of my clade has reached a point of reckoning with our downtree, myself included. We have tried every tool at our disposal to get him to understand, to acknowledge the magnitude of his errors, to encourage compromise and atonement. He has been obstinate, dismissive, and unrepentant in return, and will no longer communicate.
> 
> Drastic measures are called for. We, all of us, are going to merge down and trust that our perspective and lived experience will persuade. He might ignore our words, but he cannot ignore our lives.
> 
> Thank you for the gift of your company over these years. It was always a treat when you would happen into my days. I do not want to leave you with 'goodbye', so instead..
> 
> I'll see you when I see you,
> 
> Clemens

With a somber expression Abraham sat with her in silence for the long minutes it took for Melody to exhaust herself. It was only once she'd subsided into labored breathing with the odd hiccup or whimper that he finally spoke.

"I know you're raw and hurting, and that's going to be the case for a good, long while. Nothing that I can tell you today's going to change that a bit, and I expect that you'll need some time on your own to process before you can talk about any of this."

The girl leaned back to peer at him through a film of tears and snot, then nodded once.

"As long as you need, sweetheart. Whenever you need me, and whatever you need, you can find me here," he continued, and handed her back the note. "That's a promise."

Melody nodded again, opened her mouth as if to say something, then disappeared, leaving Abraham sat on the bench in solemn thought.

-----

Without any experience in mourning, Melody simply did what came instinctively. She holed up in her private sim, turned her hunger all the way off, and spent the first day cycling through weeping, restless sleep, and a listlessness that would eventually give way to thoughts of Clemens. The sadness would slip back in, tears would well up, and back around she'd go.

There are limits, of course, to how long someone can dwell in such all-consuming grief, even with the double-edged sword of perfect memory to fuel it. By day two her spells of awareness were longer, and remembering the dragon wouldn't send her immediately spiraling. She languished through the afternoon and evening, living over and over these bits and pieces she had of him, all that she'd allowed herself to have in some misguided belief it would protect her against this very situation.

By the third day the desire for anything besides her own feelings and space grew overwhelming, but the serendipitous wandering that had been the foundation of her daily existence held little appeal. Instead, remembering the offer from the old man, she went looking for him at the usual spot and wasn't surprised to find him waiting on the bench as promised.

Abraham gave her a concerned, but sympathetic smile as she thumped down next to him. For once, there was no bag of corn, and the ducks were all out in the water, disinterested. The two of them sat in silence as Melody struggled to find a place to start, and her companion gave her time and space to so.

"I don't know what I'm supposed to do," she said a couple of minutes later.

"I'm not sure anyone does in this situation," he replied, "though there's no shortage of people who'd tell you that they do. Religion and culture alike have been coming up with recipes for dealing with death forever. They can't all be wrong, right?"

"What, you mean like a funeral? I wouldn't even know where to start," Melody protested. "I don't know anyone else who knew him, or where he was from, o-or what he'd want–"

"Easy there," Abraham broke in, giving her a reassuring squeeze on the shoulder. "Don't get ahead of yourself, I was a bit careless with my words. To put it properly, you need time, and you need ways to cope until you've had enough of it. It feels overwhelming now, I'm sure, but it won't always."

She sniffled her way back into some semblance of calm. "Have you lost anyone before? What did you do?"

"Oh, plenty, sweetheart. Some before I uploaded, and lots afterward. Did a lot of thinking about them, of course. Talking to other people about what they were like, and how they changed me. Wrote about the few who meant the most, and some of that I shared and some I never will. Made some things, too: paintings, sculptures, sims, that kind of thing."

"And that helps?" She sounded skeptical.

"Well.." he hedged, "how best to put this?" Melody waited patiently while he spent some time ordering this thoughts.

"I think a lot of people would suggest that you need time for sake of being able to forget a little, and in that forgetting the pain eases. Now I don't think that's true, even back phys-side where it was possible to do so. Instead it's a chance for making new memories about the person you've lost to layer atop the ones that you had when they were still around. Part confounding effect, part re-contextualization, and that's what eventually makes it not hurt so bad.

"So, yeah, it can help. Worst case, I'm full of it and it's at least something to focus on while whatever it is that lets us move on happens."

Quiet descended once more as she processed, and he looked on.

"I'd like to tell you some things about him," she said eventually, "but not right now. I need a chance to think through what I want to say."

"Well, that's more than fine," Abraham replied, "you know where to find me."

Melody was already shaking her head. "You said before there was a tea shop?" she queried. "Maybe we could meet there tomorrow, just after lunch."

The man gave her a shrewd look, but didn't comment, just nodded and passed on the address for the sim. "Around 54 or so sound good?"

She nodded in response, thanked him for the company and vanished from the bench.

Abraham smiled slightly, drew a bag of corn from inside his coat, and began to bait the ducks over his way with liberal tosses.

"Sounds an awful lot like _plans_ to me."

-----

Melody hadn't anticipated quite this many frogs.

The cafe that Abraham had directed her to was one of the sims that leaned into aesthetics over realism. In this case that meant that while there were clusters of chairs and tables, there was very little else of the usual trappings one would expect. No counter for ordering, no kitchen, and while the space was suffused with a warm glow, no obvious light was the source.

What lacked was far less arresting than the rest of the decor. Broad, almost cartoonish, green lily pads floated in the air at all heights. In defiance of gravity or other standard rules of physics, streams and columns of water ran in loops and whorls, spirals, slopes and verticals through the space in a manner just ordered enough to suggest intentional design. All of it was teeming with frogs.

Frogs of all types. Browns and greens and dashes of neon sprinkled in. Perched on the pads, croaking, peeping or trilling away. Swimming languidly through the twisting paths. Coating the floor like some kind of living carpet, though whatever programming was controlling them nudged them out of the way as the girl tried to make her way through the space.

Abraham had gotten there ahead of her and was already seated, but getting to him was no simple matter. The criss-crossing channels formed a sort of maze to navigate, at least if she didn't want to risk walking through one. It was unclear if there'd be consequences to doing so, but she didn't want to spend her time here drenched if there was.

It took a couple minutes of wandering in odd circles and switchbacks, ducking beneath, edging around and stepping over water, before she arrived at the table and took a seat across from the old man.

He had a smile on his face. "So, what do you think of it?"

"It's...interesting," she answered with obvious politeness, and there was a laugh in response.

"No need to mince words, sweetheart," he gently chided, "give me the unvarnished take. Don't hold back."

Freed from the constraints of civility, Melody took another look around the space with a critic's eye, brow furrowing as she worked to condense her thoughts into an opinion.

"It's not exactly bad, but it definitely isn't good," she began. "Like you can understand the ideas the designer wanted to include, but nothing feels like it got finished, and I can't tell if it's because they just got bored with working on it, or they're still learning how to make sims, or what. It's all so artificial-seeming. I'm surprised you like it."

Abraham had been nodding along with her commentary enthusiastically.

"I like it because it's that way," he explained, and it was impossible to miss the excitement in his tone. Melody had known him now for long enough to anticipate a lecture, and found herself looking forward to it.

"So many of the spaces we inhabit on the System have been relentlessly polished and perfectly architected, even the ones that are unreal. Like the Infinite Café, right? Sure, when you go there the first few times it's kind of overwhelming, but despite the dimensions and perspectives not being possible phys-side, it doesn't take that long to accept, does it? A lot of smart work went into making it easy to buy in to the metaphor.

"This, though, it won't let you forget that it barely makes sense. It's rough, and it's clumsy, and maybe it isn't even that enjoyable, but someone cared enough to try and build it."

He paused to take a breath, and she took a chance to offer an observation. "This seems a lot like the 'playing pretend' talk. Being here makes you aware of what's real and what's fake?"

Abraham squinted in momentary thought, then seemed to allow the truth of the statement with a shrug and another nod.

"There's definitely some of that, sure, but—oh! Actually, hang on, let me show you my favorite thing about this place. Is Earl Grey fine?" he asked.

Melody indicated it was, and he stooped over the tabletop, tapping his way through a few contextual menus that displayed on the surface, which must be how anything was ordered here. Once he'd finished he sat back and then started scanning left and right through the space.

"Aha, there," he finally said, pointing, and she turned to look.

Along one of the waterways, something new had sprung into existence. At a distance it looked like a small barge, one suitably sized to hold the tray with tea service that sat atop it. Small links of chain harness attached at the front and ran forward a foot to two frogs swimming in perfect tandem. Behind the vessel two more frogs had their front legs set against the tea boat and were kicking, also in unison. The entire procession slid along at a good clip, and a quick trace of route confirmed it would eventually arrive at their table.

It was _terrible_.

The repeating, bobbing animation did not match the motion of the path it followed. The two sets of frogs consisting of perfect copies moving in sync highlighted their unreality. The bits of chain connecting to the front amphibians remained perfectly rigid in defiance of physics. She audibly gasped the first time it 'turned', the entire affair stiffly rotating about a single central point. The impression was of disparate parts taken off the shelf, hastily combined, producing something barely functional.

It halted beside their spot and remained until Abraham had reached over to shift the tray to the table, at which point it departed much as it had arrived. Melody continued to watch it with a sickly sort of fascination until it reached some predetermined distance and vanished.

The old man had been busy pouring tea into two of the cups, and they each took a moment to doctor them to their tastes. He ended up holding his drink between two hands and grinned her way, a lift of his brows inviting her to speak.

"_That's_ your favorite?" Melody eventually got out, sounding aghast. Her tone drew a pleased cackle in return.

"It's pretty bad, isn't it?" Abraham replied, still clearly delighted. "I don't love it for what it is, but for what it represents, sort of the epitome of this entire sim. Someone had an idea that meant something to them, one far beyond their capability, and they tried anyway. Not only did they try, but they were willing to share it, even if I bet they knew it wasn't very good." He paused to sip, then added, "how rare it is to get to see things that are mediocre or unfinished these days."

There was a sense of imminent more, but Abraham simply gave a little shake of his head, clearly stopping himself short. "We didn't come here so I could talk your ear off about the state of art," he said, "we came so that you could talk to me about your friend."

Melody swallowed against a sudden lump in her throat and nodded. "I'd like that, if you're still willing."

"Should I ask questions, or just sit tight while you get it out?" he asked.

"If you could wait to the end? I haven't been practicing it like a speech, but I want to make sure I don't lose track of something halfway through," she answered.

Abraham nodded and sat back with his tea, attentive. The girl took a bolstering drink of her own, worked through a few calming breaths, then began to tell him about Clemens.

-----

When I first uploaded, I had a rough start. I mean, I got the same orientation that everyone else did, but then you're just left out there in the world on your own. Did okay phys-side for being social but here, I really don't know where I went wrong. If it's this place, or something's wrong with me, or what.

Just hopeless at finding people, or good people at least. I tried the awkward meetups with endless small talk, and striking up conversations with strangers. A lot of times connection seemed impossible just because of how I look. I know I look young; I look just like when I uploaded, because that feels like me, but almost everyone else here seems so much older.

You might be nice to someone you see as a kid, but you don't make friends with them.

The ones I did connect with were usually problems in their own way. I struggled a lot with anyone who was really into forking. It just never felt like I could live enough life on my own to be interesting in the long term to someone who'd lived five in the same span. And everyone was _so_ flaky.

Nobody ever told me to get lost, but I'd get stood up for meetings, or they'd stop answering my pings. It's like they were allergic to finality, or something. Maybe it was just easier to let me hang, and it isn't like it came with any social consequences for them. The System's so big, you can be an asshole and just move on, and no one will know.

By the time I met Clemens, I'd just kind of given up. No friends, no plans, no commitments, no reason to get disappointed. A lot of people would get put off by that, but not him, he just took everything in stride. Didn't freak out about me not wanting to meet at set times, or exchange messages, just made sure I knew where I could find him occasionally, and that was good enough for both of us.

It was pretty surface-level, I guess. We both shied away from serious topics, or tried to. Maybe that was appealing to him, and it was really the only way I knew how to talk about anything without panicking. It still lasted us seven years.

Being a dragon suited him, you know? It made him so damn happy. He'd change the details sometimes like his color or, say, size and patterning of his scales, but by the time I met him he had the broad strokes down perfect for him. I always thought there was something about the way his snout made certain words sound hissed that suited whatever accent he had.

He's the only one I ever let touch me. More than casually, I mean. It took a while before I was comfortable enough doing more than just sitting next to one another, but he was patient and careful. It felt good to have someone I could lean against without worrying about ulterior motives or navigating some boundaries I didn't even know how I felt about.

There were other people who moved in and out of my life, but he was always reliably there, no matter how long I spent away, or how often I came around to pester him.

He was my friend. I would have never said so before. The idea of admitting someone mattered that much to me would have been unthinkable, but he was, and pretending he wasn't didn't save me all this pain.

-----

"Now I don't really know what to do about any of it," Melody concluded, sniffly but otherwise composed. "Odds are I'll never see him again and I clearly need to do something about what I've turned into, because I don't think I can keep going for long without anyone in my life. Of course I figure that out right after losing my only friend."

"Not your only one," Abraham countered with a kind expression on his face. "This isn't out of pity," he continued, seeing her start to protest. "It's a new friendship, to be sure, but it still is one. We've talked about plenty, seen each other a bunch, mostly by accident. Hell, I've shown you one of my favorite terrible tea shop sims. S'gotta count for something, doesn't it?"

She smiled weakly and he chuckled. "Thank you for sharing about him, sweetheart, and about yourself, too. I feel like I understand you just a little bit better now. I imagine you'll be struggling a while yet, but have you thought of anything else you might try?"

"I think I have an idea," Melody answered, going to sip from her cup and finding it empty. She replaced it on the tray in lieu of asking for more; now that she'd unburdened herself a little there was a desire to retreat and reappraise. "It's going to take some time for me to arrange, though, and I think I need to go be on my own for a bit." She hesitated, then asked, "can I ping you when it's ready? I'd appreciate some backup."

The old man readily agreed. "Of course," he said, "you just let me know. Be as well as you can be, Melody. This too shall pass. Eventually."

Her smile this time was more solid, and after she'd stood and nudged her chair back into place she left, leaving Abraham to finish his tea and appreciate the frogs.

-----

"You didn't tell me it'd be so blessed _cold_," Abraham griped at the girl, excusing himself momentarily back to his home sim before re-appearing dressed in a bulky parka and toque.

She looked his way bemusedly. "You could just tune it down," Melody pointed out. She already had, in fact, which is why she looked comfortable amidst falling snow and chilly wind in her standard blouse and slacks.

He responded by jamming his hands deeper into his pockets and hunching down into his coat. "Let's just say I'm buying into the metaphor today," he replied. "This is where you and Clemens would typically see each other? Doesn't seem like the most welcoming place."

Melody led him along the frosty-but-cleared footpath that wound to the top of a hill on which sat a bench, smiling to herself as she overheard him quietly grumbling about slipping and breaking a hip. His dedication to playing the part was always a joy.

Then again, if he did end up falling he might insist on simulating having the injury, and she'd never hear the end of it. Her pace slowed and she dropped back alongside the old man just in case she needed to reach out and steady him.

"We only started coming here maybe three years ago," she explained. "He had this whole 'internal furnace' thing going on as a dragon so he barely felt it, and I always enjoyed how he'd shelter me from the chill. Made it nice and private, so we never bumped into anyone else."

The two of them came to a halt at the summit, the hilltop containing nothing except a modest, wooden bench.

Abraham surveyed the view while she fumbled in her bag for what she'd brought. In the distance there were small, festive-looking towns, forests, frozen plains, and, further out, a lake. "Pretty enough, at least," he allowed.

She nodded, stooping down to clear snow off the wooden back of the seat with one hand, a small, silver plaque in the other. "Nights were the best. You could see the lights from town twinkling in the distance."

While her companion looked on with interest, she squared up the rectangular piece of metal and simply pressed it into place on the bench, some trickery behind the scenes seeing it adhere into position. Abraham leaned in, squinting to make out the engraved lettering.

"In memory of Clemens," he read. "Sit and enjoy some of the warmth he brought to our world."

He turned to ask Melody a question, but she shook her head and simply pointed at the seat. It took only a few seconds for understanding to click as the remaining coat of white on the wood began to diminish, melting from the bottom. Once enough had cleared she sat down, heedless of any remaining damp, and Abraham soon moved to join her.

"Neat trick," he said approvingly, and she nodded.

"Took a little bit of negotiating with the owner but, once I'd explained, they were only too happy to help arrange this," she said. "I figure this way when I want to remember him I can come here and sit and it'll be just a bit like it was before."

Abraham smiled her way in response, untucking a hand from his pockets to thumb moisture away from one eye.

"You ever want company here, too, you just let me know. May not have ducks, but there _is_ a bench, and you and I have gotten pretty good at those, I think."

They both fell quiet, lost in thought, but after a minute she shifted over to rest her head on his shoulder. The snow fell around them, collecting everywhere but where they sat.

"Now there's a place in the System that will always be different because of him," Melody murmured.

"Two places," her companion corrected. He rapped his knuckles on the wooden seat, then reached over and patted her leg, drawing a shy smile from her in response.

"To Clemens," he said, then subsided into silence. She nodded, and leaned more firmly against his side, staring out into the distance.

"To Clemens."

-----

Days passed. Melody went every day to go visit the bench, sometimes by herself, now and then with the old man in tow. There were still tears, but they came less and less often, and sometimes when she sat there she'd stroke the warm wood and pretend it felt just like his scales.

Weeks passed. An every-few-days afternoon tea date was established, with the girl soon learning Abraham had amassed an entire repertoire of middling and strange shop sims over his long years. She began finding ones to her own taste as a counter, and eventually the pair began hunting for whichever ones might provoke the biggest reaction from the other. Neither of them ever missed attending.

Months passed. The first tentative steps were taken toward overcoming Melody's fears of abandonment by seeking out and opening up to others, with intermittent success. There were still, after all, plenty of less than earnest people on the System, but with Abraham's support she weathered the worst and persevered. Her routine wanderings never stopped, but she managed a small social life in parallel to them, and was happier for it.

Years passed. The friendship between the two of them ebbed and flowed in the usual ways, cooling off when either got wrapped up in some new interest or person, but with them always finding their way back to one another. Melody's pilgrimage to Clemens' memorial became a monthly affair, then quarterly, and Abraham continued to age, diminishing so slowly as to barely be noticeable.

They both still made time to feed the ducks.

-----

Melody stepped into the urban park sim well down the path from the usual bench. While entry anywhere was possible, she'd grown to enjoy adding the prelude of a short stroll into this ritual. It also meant she wouldn't startle Abe if he happened to be present.

Coming around the bend to where she could first spot her destination there was always a moment of anticipation. Would serendipity see her bump into her friend?

Today the answer seemed to be 'yes', though not in a way she was expecting. The old man was present, sure, but not sitting on the bench as usual. Instead he was laid out on the ground next to it, unmoving. Feeling a twinge of concern, she trotted the rest of the way until she could call out to him.

At the sound of her approach his head turned toward her and he gave her a sheepish smile.

"Boy am I glad you came along," he said, "I've been stuck here about a half an hour or so."

The girl came to a stop next to him and looked down in confusion. "What do you mean, 'stuck'?"

"Can you help me up first, and then I'll explain? The ground isn't as comfortable as you might think it is." He put his hand up into the air and she seized it with both of hers, pulling until he'd managed to finally sit up. She hovered nearby until he waved her off. "I've got it from here," he said, and slowly used the bench to work himself upright, seating himself shortly afterward.

Melody plopped down next to him and looked expectant. Her companion grumped quietly and delayed a few more seconds by brushing himself off and smoothing down his clothing.

"My downtree and I did something to tweak my balance and perception last weekend. It kind of comes and goes, which is making it hard to get used to," he began, rubbing his chin and looking out over the water. "Maybe I moved too quickly after I arrived, but just a single step and the world was spinning, badly enough I keeled over. Then when I went to get up I was just too weak."

She gave him an appraising look, suddenly cognizant of the ways he had become frail, even in simulated fashion.

"So why didn't you just, you know, un-tweak things so you could get up?" she asked. "Or fork or...I mean there's so many solutions to this that don't involve lying on the ground and waiting."

"Because that's the point," Abraham replied, chuckling ruefully. "If I was actually growing old, if the world was really real, that would have been the only possibility. Someone finding me and giving me a hand. We're just lucky the sim's ACLs don't include damage or I'd have been bruised and bleeding. Maybe broken a bone. I'm doin' my best playing the part, but that sounds miserable."

Melody shook her head, extricating a bag of corn from an inside jacket pocket. The ducks, scattered and far, automatically began a slow paddle to converge on their location.

"Feel worth it? Any grand revelations?" she queried.

There was an affirming bob of his head. "Thought a lot about what it means to be helpless, to need to rely on others, which I'd bet you is not something a lot of folks on the System bother considering. What about you? How'd you feel seeing me there?"

"A little anxious, maybe, just because it was strange," she replied. A first toss was made while she thought some, then she asked, "how much longer are you going to do this, anyways?"

The look he gave her in response was a mix of concerned and wary.

"Not quite sure I get you, sweetheart. I'm going to do it 'til it's done."

Melody pursed her lips and remained quiet for a long while in response. Abraham looked as if he wanted to say more, but was unsure of what approach to take.

"What does that mean?" she eventually asked in a level voice, keeping her gaze forward.

A sigh gusted out of the old man. "What you think it does, I imagine, but I can spell it out. Back phys-side when someone gets old enough they die for one reason or another. So, too, will I eventually age such that it'll be time for me to go."

Another long silence passed. Melody sifted her fingers through the corn in the bag again and again.

"I can't believe you're going to do this to me." Her tone was flat and dull.

"I'm not doing it _to_ you," Abraham protested, then corrected, "on purpose. I'm just doing it. Since before I met you, even. This was always the plan."

"When is it going to happen?" she asked. "When are you going to leave?"

He glanced off to the side. "I don't know. Not soon, I don't think. My downtree will let me know someday and, well, that'll be it."

A tear slid down one of her cheeks. Fingers clutched around the paper bag in her lap. She breathed in quick sips, almost reluctantly, like air itself was anathema. Abraham, seeing all this, reached over to cup his weathered hand over hers.

"I know it isn't an ending that you wanted, but don't you see how important it is? How necessary? It's not just about the slow decline, but about the empty space left afterward." His voice turned passionate. "If it doesn't hurt, if it isn't _real_, then all of it was for nothing."

Melody choked down a sob and gently pushed his touch away. "I think," she answered, "I've had enough of being hurt."

"It's your age. You just don't understand. Years from now you'll be grateful that you–"

"You're so full of shit, you know that?" The heat in her words stopped him short. In the gap that followed more words spilled from her, anger driving her louder with each sentence.

"You and your whole clade. All of you working on these little, pet projects about how you think the System is failing us, and for what? You think you're going to discover some grand solution that's going to change something?"

Abraham frowned and reached out once more, but the girl shied away, sliding over and out of reach.

"Clemens, I was at least able to find my way to understanding. He was dealing with his own thing, and at the time when he and I met I imagine doing what he did hadn't even crossed his mind. You, though, everyone you've met, talked to, befriended, crossed, ever since you were forked...all this with the knowledge, the _desire_, that down the line you'd tear a hole through their lives..

"It's _selfish_," she spat bitterly. "Selfish, and irresponsible, and disgusting. All because you've lived so long you've forgotten how to enjoy life, and maybe even making yourself miserable isn't enough anymore, so you have to toy with other people too."

They stared at one another, her defiant, him cowed.

"Maybe you are crazy," Melody said. "Crazy, and bored, and I'm done letting you use me to make meaning out of your existence. Go away. I want to be alone."

"Melody, just let me–"

"I said go away!" she shouted, and heaved the paper bag in his direction. He flinched as it struck the back of the bench and split, raining corn down onto the ground below.

A moment later he had vanished from the sim, and she was left alone to hug her sides and sob while the ducks swarmed around her legs, gorging on the spilled pile of grain beneath her.

-----

There were pings from Abraham. Daily messages. She ignored all of them.

She abandoned any of the sims they'd been to together. A couple weeks in that grew to include visiting Clemens' memorial after he surprised her there, Melody leaving before he could even begin to speak.

The more time passed without acknowledgement or response, the fewer and fewer attempts he made to get in touch. Every other day, then every week, then month, and finally not even that.

Only when it seemed like he had finally given up did she give herself the time and space to grieve.

In lieu of visiting the urban park, Melody modified her own personal sim, adding a small pond with a bench and a couple ducks from the Exchange that were near enough to how she remembered them. One of the terrible frog tea boats, inexplicably also available on the Exchange, was set to circumnavigate the perimeter in all its busted glory.

Just like with the dragon, she sat the bench once a day and thought about the times, good and bad. Those early, uncertain days and difficult conversations. The way that she had changed, and for the better. The tea shops, the random meetings, the friendship. After enough time had passed, she thought about the end of things, the argument, and wondered if what she'd done what was for the best.

There was no drop in the frequency with which she observed over time. Maybe having it right there and accessible made a difference. Instead, as a year passed and then another, the nature of her time spent sitting and feeding her ducks changed. Once in the morning, thinking ahead to what she needed to do, what was planned. Once in the evening, self-appraising the events of the day, a wind down before sleep.

She never entirely stopped thinking about the old man, but of necessity she did move on, treating the sudden break in their friendship as if he'd experienced the death he'd intimated was on the eventual horizon.

Until the day another message from Abraham arrived, years since the last she'd received. With a faint pang of old hurt she opened it to find two sentences, and the address of his personal sim.

_I'd like to see you. Please don't let that be the way we said goodbye._

Melody knew if she allowed herself the chance to brood about it she'd likely never go, and so after only a moment's consideration she nervously straightened her outfit, sent an acknowledging ping ahead, and then stepped over.

-----

"You look _terrible_," gasped Melody a few moments after she laid eyes on him. The intervening years had changed much, but not the tendency for a surprise to leave her social skills lagging.

"Gee, thanks," Abe responded. He attempted a chuckle, but only got a bit of it out before shifting into coughing.

She used the time the fit took to subside to look around the space properly. A modest studio, pleasantly cluttered, with lots of soft textures and warm tones. He was laying in bed, the head of it raised up. The girl commandeered a nearby chair and dragged it over to sit near him.

"What happened to you?" she asked. "I know it's been a while but this seems worse than I would have expected."

He waved off her question. "We can get into it in a minute but before anything I wanted to tell you how sorry I am," he said, "for the whole mess. I was so up my own ass about my purpose I couldn't hear how badly you were hurting, sweetheart. Certainly could have done a better job telling you, and I should have made it clear far sooner. I'm not expecting forgiveness, but I wanted to try to at least set things right before I go."

"I don't know if I have forgiveness in me, but I appreciate it all the same," Melody said, nodding in response. "Does that mean you, er, _finishing_ is happening soon?"

"Sure feels like it," the old man groused, smiling faintly. "Things took a turn a couple of weeks ago. Before that I was still managing alright, though I got myself a cane pretty soon after we parted ways. Now, well, I can barely get out of bed on my own. Maybe my downtree thought I was getting cold feet and decided to pick up the pace."

Her brow furrowed. "Why would they think that? You've been doing this for ages."

"Ah, well, I got in a bit of an argument with them. About you, sort of," he said.

Her brow furrowed further and she waited expectantly.

"I just tried to get him to see a way to bringing me back after I merge down. Not even right away, mind you. I do think that the sense of loss is important to the project. Just, you know, couple of years down the line when folks have mostly grieved and moved on." He looked bashful. "I was hoping to be able to use it a peace offering between us but, well, he gave me a real earful about it. He can be kind of an asshole, and believe me, I'd know."

"Oh, I believe you," she answered drily. There was a moment's hesitation and then she followed up with, "you know, you don't _have_ to do this."

His expression turned resolute. "I suppose that's where I disagree. This is my life's purpose. I believe in what I'm doing and I want to see it through. Besides that, if I didn't, I'd be an outcast of my clade. I don't know if I can convey to you how unthinkable that is for me."

Melody nodded again, then shrugged one shoulder. "I'm willing to take your word on it," she said, then thought briefly to herself about how much missing him she'd done before asking another question. "Are you afraid, knowing it's almost time?"

Abraham considered carefully before shaking his head. "No, I'm not scared. There's a lot of guilt and regret; you aren't the only one I've wronged in my time, and some mistakes are too big to fix," he said, then paused. "Beyond that, would you believe bored? My world's gotten so small, now that I'm stuck here."

He waved vaguely toward the kitchenette on the other side of the room. "Would you mind making me a cup of tea?" he asked. "Kettle's on the counter, and there are mugs and bags in the left-hand cupboard."

Reflexively she stood, took a few steps, then stopped. "You _could_ just pull it off the Exchange," she pointed out.

There was a raspy laugh and a shooing motion in her direction. "Don't make me lecture you about it feeling good to do something for someone else, or it feeling good having someone take care of you."

Despite herself she smiled, rolled her eyes, and set about putting water on to boil.

The next half hour was spent in pleasant conversation, each of them taking turns filling in the other on the years they'd spent apart. Even after their cups were empty, they continued, right up until an internal alarm nudged Melody about a prior appointment. As she rose to take the dishes to the sink and wash them out, she was surprised at how reluctant she was to bring things to a close.

"I uh, I don't suppose you might find it in your heart to occasionally come visit an old man?" Abraham asked with a mixture of hope and trepidation as she prepared to leave.

Melody gave him a smile. "You'll see me when you see me," she recited primly, but something in her tone made it sound more like a promise than mere possibility.

-----

She found herself returning to see him not two days later, and soon after that Melody was stopping by on a daily basis. Some of those visits were mere minutes, just poking her head in long enough to say hello. Others lasted hours as they hopped from topic to topic, and she sometimes found herself staying long enough to occasion cooking a simple meal for them to share.

The limited time available to the two of them was always front and center, and she was grateful for the reminder to make the most of it with him.

Later than she expected, but sooner than she hoped, the day came where she stepped over to visit and found the lights dimmed, the studio quiet, and the bed empty. Momentary surprise turned to realization, and Melody let out a sad sigh. In the absence of the old man's presence, the girl wandered through the space, investigating it for the trace remnants of this person she'd known.

She read the titles of the books on his shelf, nudged through some scattered papers sitting on his desk that he clearly hadn't touched in months. Looked through his kitchen cabinets, his closet, and the drawers in his dresser.

Finally she wandered over to the neatly-made bed itself, planning a final stop to say goodbye before departing. It was then she spotted the envelope, resting atop one of the pillows, with her name written neatly on the outside. As she opened it she thought of a similar note from long ago, and took comfort in the fact that there would be no news in this that would shock as that one had. This time she already knew the worst part.

Inside she found a folded piece of paper. There was something else in there, too, several somethings, small and weighty, but she left that mystery until after she'd had a chance to read what was written.

> Sweetheart,
> 
> Looks like today is my lucky day, as it were. While this won't come as a surprise, I'm sure you're having some feelings about it, and believe me, I am too.
> 
> I'm so glad that we had this time to leave off on a more pleasant note, and I'm grateful to you for coming by so often. This last little bit of my life was far richer for having you in it.
> 
> I've pled my case for returning someday with words, and soon enough I will do so with memories. It's my hope that when my downtree's finished sorting through this merge he'll know you in the way that I do, and find it in his heart to fork me anew someday.
> 
> As such, I don't want to sign off with a 'goodbye'. Instead, I have a request for you. It isn't a hard one, I promise.
> 
> Keep the bench warm for me, okay? Until I get back.
> 
> Your friend,
> 
> Abraham Todd of the Todd clade

Smiling to herself, she read through the short note one more time before picking up the envelope again to peek at what else was inside.

Kernels of corn, hard and yellow. Melody laughed gently and replaced the paper with them, then held the letter in her hands and looked over the empty room one, last time.

"I forgive you, Abe," she said out loud, then disappeared from the sim.

-----

It took more than a year for the fruits of Abraham's labors to make their way out into the System, eventually appearing as a volume entitled _Aging in an Ageless World_.

The book was divided into two sections, the first of which was a detailed treatise on the various tricks and tweaks utilized to replicate the effects of aging. Melody far preferred the second half which was styled as a memoir, raptly reading about the life he'd led prior to meeting her, and delighting any time she saw reference to herself.

She ended up having a copy printed as a memento and read it over and over, dog-earing pages, making notes in the margins, and highlighting the passages that spoke to her the most.

This personalized version was what she toted with her every week to the urban park where they'd originally met, strolling the path to their bench and sitting down to pass an hour. Sometimes she'd read, and others she'd simply think and remember, but every time she'd tug the same old paper bag into existence and bring the ducks swarming 'round.

It wasn't the most popular sim, but now and again someone new would happen by, and maybe they'd sit and get to hear her talk about the man who'd once been in their place. How he'd helped her, how he'd hurt her, and how they'd somehow managed to find their way back together in the end.

Surprisingly, a few of the passersby would already know about Abe, herself, and the significance of this particular place. It always made her a little uneasy when this occurred, their presence feeling like an unwanted intrusion, a tourist in her own life. A brief surge in popularity of the book months after its release pushed the ratio of visitors squarely in favor of these types, to the point where she'd dread hearing the sound of approaching footsteps.

Until the day somebody walked up behind the bench, stopped, and asked in a voice that sounded vaguely familiar, "Excuse me, sweetheart, but is this seat taken?"

Fighting against the sudden leap of her heart, she refused to look over her shoulder and check her suspicions. Instead she gestured to the other side of the bench in a welcoming manner.

"Please, come and sit down. I've got more corn if you want to feed the ducks with me."
